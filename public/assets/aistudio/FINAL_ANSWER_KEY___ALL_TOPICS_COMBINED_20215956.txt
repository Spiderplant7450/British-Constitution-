1. NATURE AND CONTENT OF THE CONSTITUTION


1.1 Meaning of Constitution


· General meaning: Written, precise, systematic document; supreme law; special amendment procedure; statutory law must be consistent with letter and spirit; otherwise unconstitutional/ultra vires.
· Britain: No written/codified constitution; undefined, unsystematised, undecided; lacks precision and coherence; no single document; scattered enactments; greater part unwritten (custom).
· Constitutional law = statutory law: Same source, same procedure; no judicial review; courts cannot set aside Parliament's enactments.
· Unwritten and flexible: Product of history and evolution; "child of wisdom and chance"; Jennings: "not made but grown—no paper."
· Paine & Tocqueville: Said British Constitution did not exist — WRONG; no State without constitution; no constitution wholly written or entirely unwritten; difference is degree, not kind.
· Bryce: Written constitutions "developed by interpretation, fringed with decisions, enlarged by customs."
· US: 7 Articles, 10 pages; India: 395 Articles, 12 Schedules.
· No constitution complete by itself: Framework/skeleton filled by detailed rules, customs, judicial decisions, amendments.


1.2 Nature: Body of Basic Rules


· Same in nature as other constitutions; only difference: never systematised/codified.
· Dynamic: Ever-growing; roots in past, branches in future.
· Practical: Expediency, not theory; matter-of-fact.
· Lacks logic/symmetry (Ogg): "Lacks symmetry, abounds in the illogical" — but not a hotchpotch; rules deduced from British experience and consciously applied.
· Anson: "Rambling structure"; "convenient rather than symmetrical"; divergence of law and custom, theory and practice.
· Bagehot: Prerequisites — mutual confidence among electors, calm national mind, gift of rationality; adult and practical nation; codified constitution "more trouble than worth."

2. SOURCES OF THE CONSTITUTION (7 Categories)


# Source Key Points
1 Charters, Petitions, Statutes Magna Carta 1215, Petition of Rights 1628, Bill of Rights 1689, Act of Settlement 1701, Parliament Act 1911/1949, Statute of Westminster 1931, Indian Independence Act 1947; product of political crisis; Elder William P.: "Bible of the British Constitution"
2 Ordinary Statutes Suffrage, elections, public officials; not crisis-driven; passed as needed; vitally important for democracy; repeal against "constitutional sense"; system would become unworkable; Parliament sovereign ("make or unmake any law")
3 Judicial Decisions Interpret/define/develop Charters and Statutes; create precedents; Sheriff of Middlesex (1840): Parliament can punish members for breach of privilege; Bradlaugh v. Gossett (1884): Parliament supreme over courts in internal affairs
4 Common Law King's prerogative; Parliament's supremacy; civil liberties (speech, press, assembly, home, jury trial); based on usage; Blackstone: customs "not set down in any written statute... depending on immemorial usages"; judges recognised customs, set precedents; "bulwark of British freedom"; continually developing
5 Usages and Conventions Centre and soul of constitutional law; fundamental convention = Cabinet Government; Finer: rules of political behaviour not established in statutes/judicial decisions/parliamentary customs; object: make executive and legislature responsible to will of people; Jennings: rest on general acquiescence
6 Commentaries Less reliable; systematise rules; acquired status of constitutional documents; Erskine May's Treatise — classic guide to parliamentary procedure/privileges, referred to by Speakers; Dicey's Law of the Constitution — more than commentary
7 Royal Prerogative Declare war, make treaties, pardon criminals, dissolve Parliament; executed through Orders in Council, proclamations, writs under great seal; performed by Ministers on behalf of Monarch; authority from Crown, not Parliament

3. CONVENTIONS OF THE CONSTITUTION (MOST IMPORTANT)


3.1 Meaning and Place


· Dicey: indefinite number of customs, traditions, precedents; integral part; not law; nowhere written down.
· Deep-rooted; without them Constitution maimed/unworkable.
· Centre and soul of constitutional law.
· Fundamental convention: Cabinet Government.


3.2 Laws vs Conventions


Aspect Law Convention
Origin Legally constituted body Extra-legal; practice/usage
Formulation Precise terms Never formulated; modified by practice
Enforcement Courts Not courts; no legal sanction
Binding force Willingness of people General acquiescence


· No definite boundary line; fundamental conventions recognised by many Acts (British North America Act 1867, Anglo-Irish Treaty 1921, Status of Union Act 1934, Statute of Westminster 1931).
· Edward VIII abdication demonstrated convention on Succession to Throne.
· Ministers of the Crown Act 1937: recognised PM, Cabinet, Party, Opposition, Leader of Opposition; did not legalise conventions, only recognised existence.
· Jennings: conventional system much like common law system.


3.3 Sanction Behind Conventions


Thinker Explanation
Dicey Violation ultimately = breach of law (annual session → taxes → Army Act)
Lowell Dicey incomplete; Parliament can pass permanent laws; some violations cause no legal breach (Speaker not resigning from party; not recognising Opposition; PM from Lords)
Jennings Conventions exist for good reasons; obeyed due to political difficulties; government is co-operative function; habit of obedience
Neumann "Unconstitutional" = "not proper"; things simply not done by gentlemen
Dicey (moral) Constitutional morality secures sovereignty of people
Marshall & Moodie Conventions describe how legal powers must be exercised to be tolerated by those affected
Privy Council (1935) British Coal Corporation v. Rex: abstract law vs. realities; theory has no relation to realities


3.4 Kinds of Conventions


1. Parliament–Executive Harmony (Cabinet Government)


· Ministers must be MPs; belong to majority party; PM as leader.
· Collective responsibility; Cabinet responsible to Commons; resign/dissolve on losing confidence.
· 1924 Conservative Ministry: no clear majority → met Parliament; couldn't ask another dissolution.
· Summon Parliament in crisis; Commons consulted on war/treaties.


2. Legislative Procedure / Two Houses


· Parliament meets annually; two Houses; Commons' financial initiative.
· Lords' subordination (till Parliament Act 1911/1949).
· Only Law Lords sit in judicial capacity.
· Three readings of Bills; Government speech followed by Opposition.
· HM Opposition; Speaker as no-party man; retiring Speaker returned unopposed.


3. Government–Electorate (Mandate Convention)


· Controversial legislation needs electoral mandate; vindication of popular sovereignty.
· 1945 Labour nationalisation; Viscount Cranborne: "constitutionally wrong" for Lords to oppose mandated proposals.
· Applies to foreign policy too.
· Ministry must retire if electorate rejects it; no second dissolution.
· Greaves: political sanction behind conventions.


4. Dominion/Commonwealth Relations


· Statute of Westminster 1931: legal form to conventions; legislative independence of Dominions.
· King acts on Dominion Ministers' advice.
· British Parliament doesn't legislate for Dominion without consent.
· Treaty rules: Imperial Conferences 1923, 1926, 1930.
· Governor-General's position: Conferences 1926, 1930.


3.5 Why Conventions Are Observed


· Dicey: violation = ultimate breach of law (annual session example).
· Lowell: Dicey incomplete; some conventions violated without legal breach.
· Jennings: good reasons; political difficulties; government as co-operative function; habit.
· Neumann: prefer proven procedures; "unconstitutional" = "not proper"; things not done by gentlemen.
· Dicey (moral): constitutional morality → sovereignty of people.
· Marshall & Moodie: legal powers must be exercised to be tolerated.
· Privy Council 1935: abstract law vs. realities.


3.6 Role of Conventions


· Democratise Executive: Parliament as centre of gravity; Government and Opposition work together.
· Revolutionise Judiciary: Law Lords as highest Court of Civil Appeal.
· Commonwealth relations: inter-Commonwealth collaboration.
· "Flesh which clothes dry bones of law": give life to rigid legal framework.
· Motive power: lubricate machinery of government.
· Without them: structure of government would collapse.
· Finer: no written constitution can express fulness of life's meanings.
· Flexible and growing: adjusted without political stir.
· Transition: monarchy to democracy without writing new articles.


3.7 Limits of Dicey's Theory


· Dicey partially true: some conventions backed by ultimate legal sanctions.
· Dicey incomplete: not all conventions have legal sanctions; some violations cause no legal breach.
· Examples of no legal breach: Speaker not resigning from party; not recognising Opposition; PM from Lords; conduct of business not observed.
· Precedents broken: 1931 Labour "agreed to differ" (justified by Baldwin — conventions altered by circumstances); Disraeli 1868 (resigned without meeting Parliament); Baldwin 1929 (reverted to old convention).
· Jennings: conventions exist for good reasons.
· Neumann: "unconstitutional" = "not proper."
· Conclusion: Dicey partially true but incomplete.

4. SALIENT FEATURES (MOST IMPORTANT)


# Feature Key Points
1 Mostly Unwritten No single document; scattered; greater part unwritten (custom); written elements don't comprise Constitution by themselves
2 Product of Development Organic growth; McIntosh: "constitutions grow, not made"; no revolutionary upheavals; Ogg: "continuity hardly paralleled"; Morrison: peaceful evolution more permanent/satisfactory/less painful
3 Theory vs Practice Theory: absolute monarchy; form: limited constitutional monarchy; practice: democratic republic; "King can do no wrong"; "crowned republic" (Webbs); Jennings: "government by opinion"; Morrison: "takes good countries to run monarchies..."
4 Sovereignty of Parliament Supreme; make/unmake any law; no judicial review; veto obsolete; Monarch must assent; legal sovereignty
5 Flexible No special procedure; no referendum (unlike Switzerland/Australia); mandate convention; Callaghan: "not rigid and inflexible"; Ogg: psychological checks/voluntary restraints; legally most flexible, actually less fluid
6 Unitary All authority from London; local units subordinate; unlike federal USA/India; written/rigid constitution pre-requisite of federation; devolution referendum would move away from centralized state
7 Parliamentary Government Ministers from majority party; responsible to Commons; Bagehot: Cabinet = "hyphen that joins, buckle that binds"; no deadlock; Greaves: right to govern flows through legislature to Cabinet; unlike US separation of powers
8 Two-Party System Government and Opposition; coherent expression; classical example; 17th century origin; 200 years two parties; Labour 1921 (three parties); Liberal collapse; SDP 1981 (short-lived); two-party system maintained
9 Rule of Law Common Law basis; no specific rights (unlike USA/India); Lord Hewart: "supremacy/dominance of law vs arbitrariness"; equality before law; judiciary as guardian; Parliament can curtail in emergency (WWI, 1934/1936 Acts, WWII Emergency Powers Act 1939); laws promote liberty
10 Hereditary Character Monarchy hereditary; House of Lords hereditary peers; paradox with democracy; Attlee: "best example of democracy despite monarchical and oligarchical elements"

5. DEVELOPMENT OF THE CONSTITUTION


5.1 Freeman's View


· Continuous development; unbroken for 1400 years.
· No wholly new constitution; each step natural consequence of earlier step.
· Each change = development of something already old.
· Progress faster/slower, but never permanently checked.


5.2 Three Periods


Period Duration Name Key Features
First To 1485 Foundations Angles/Saxons; Norman/Angevin; Parliament showing strength; King's power eclipsed; institutional foundation laid
Second 1485–1689 Reconstruction Tudor dynasty; Stuart periods; Puritan Revolution; Commonwealth
Third 1689–present Fructification Glorious Revolution; Bill of Rights; diminished King's powers; emergence of Cabinet; rise of parties; leadership of PM; shifting power within Parliament; democratisation of Commons; Empire changes; Lords reform


5.3 Glorious Revolution 1689


· Closed great constitutional struggle of 17th century.
· Kings held throne by grace of Parliament; could be made/unmade by Parliament.
· Parliament supreme.
· Bill of Rights: limitations on King's powers; no Roman Catholic ruler or marriage to Roman Catholic; culminating point in evolution of fundamentals; centre of gravity shifted from King to Parliament.
· Seeds took time to germinate and mature.


5.4 Main Lines of Growth


1. Diminished powers of King
2. Emergence of Cabinet → responsible Ministry
3. Rise of political parties
4. Leadership of PM
5. Shifting power within Parliament (Commons supremacy)
6. Democratisation of Commons (Reform Bills from 1832)
7. Changes in British Empire
8. Labour Government reduced absolute number and hereditary element in House of Lords
