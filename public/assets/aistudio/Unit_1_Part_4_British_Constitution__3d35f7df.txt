Topic 4 — Salient Features of the British Constitution (Most Important)


4.0 Introduction


· From the nature of the Constitution flow the following important features:
  1. It is mostly unwritten
  2. A product of development and evolution
  3. Difference between theory and practice
  4. Sovereignty of Parliament
  5. Flexible Constitution
  6. Unitary Constitution
  7. Parliamentary Government
  8. Two-Party System
  9. Rule of Law and Civil Liberties
  10. Hereditary Character


---


4.1 — It Is Mostly Unwritten


Notes


· The British Constitution is to a large extent of an unwritten nature.
· It is not a pre-arranged pattern according to which government must be carried on.
· Nor is it the result of conscious creation.
· Its sources are several, and the course of its development has been:
  · sometimes guided by accident,
  · sometimes by high design.
· Being a "child of wisdom and of chance", its growth has been:
  · piecemeal, and
  · gradual.
· It has expressed itself in different:
  · Charters and Statutes,
  · precedents,
  · usages, and
  · traditions,
  · whatever the exigencies of time demanded.
· There is no single document which can be said to contain the general principles of political government.
· In fact, no attempt has ever been made to embody these principles in a documentary form.
· They remain scattered.
· Some rules and principles have been reduced to writing and embodied in Acts of Parliament, but a greater part still remains unwritten and is:
  · "simply carried in men's minds as precedents, decisions, habits, and practices."
· The written elements taken by themselves do not comprise the Constitution, though they have considerably affected it.


Key Points


Point Explanation
Mostly unwritten Large extent unwritten
Not pre-arranged Not a pattern or conscious creation
Sources several Accident and design
Child of wisdom and chance Piecemeal, gradual growth
No single document Principles scattered; no documentary embodiment
Greater part unwritten Carried in men's minds as precedents, decisions, habits, practices
Written elements Do not comprise Constitution by themselves, though they affect it

4.2 — A Product of Development and Evolution


Notes


· The British Constitution has grown like an organism and developed from age to age.
· It fulfils Sir James McIntosh's dictum that constitutions grow instead of being made.
· It is the product of evolution and the result of:
  · slow and steady development, and
  · successive accretions spreading over a thousand years.
· All through this period, Britain has never witnessed political upheavals of a revolutionary character.
· In fact, all political revolutions, if they may be described as revolutions, have been of a conservative nature.
· Britain has all through moved along an essentially continuous constitutional pathway, readjusting her institutions slowly and cautiously to the changing conditions and needs of the country and its people.
· Ogg says:
  · political changes "have as a rule been so gradual,
  · deference to traditions so habitual,
  · and the disposition to cling to accustomed names and forms even when the spirit has changed, so deep-seated,
  · that the constitutional history of Britain displays a continuity hardly paralleled in any other land."
· Lord Morrison is of the opinion that such a peaceful evolution is:
  · **"more permanent,
  · more satisfactory, and
  · less painful,
  · than if it were the result of the violent bloody revolution,"**
  · and he thinks his "country has been lucky in that respect."


Key Points


Point Explanation
Organic growth Grown like an organism from age to age
McIntosh's dictum Constitutions grow instead of being made
Product of evolution Slow, steady development; successive accretions over 1000 years
No revolutionary upheavals Revolutions conservative in nature
Continuous pathway Slow, cautious readjustment
Ogg Gradual changes, habitual deference to tradition, continuity hardly paralleled
Lord Morrison Peaceful evolution more permanent, satisfactory, less painful than violent revolution

4.3 — Difference Between Theory and Practice


Notes


· The gradualness of constitutional evolution and the English habits of retaining traditional forms, despite radical changes in the position of power, have produced a marked difference in theory and practice.
· The government in the United Kingdom:
  · in ultimate theory is an absolute Monarchy;
  · in form, a limited constitutional Monarchy;
  · in actual character, a democratic republic.


In Theory (Legal Position)


· The government is vested in the Monarch.
· All officers of the State, civil and military, are appointed and dismissed in Her Majesty's name.
· The Ministers are Her Majesty's Ministers and remain in office during the Royal pleasure.
· The Monarch is the source of law and fountain of justice.
· Her Majesty summons, dissolves and prorogues Parliament.
· No parliamentary election can be held without the Royal writ.
· Laws made by Parliament are not valid and cannot be enforced without the Royal assent.
· The Monarch may veto any law passed by Parliament.
· The Monarch is the Commander-in-Chief of all British forces during peace and war.
· War is declared in Her Majesty's name; peace and treaties are negotiated and concluded in the name and on behalf of the Monarch.
· Government documents are published by Her Majesty's Stationery Office.
· All people are the loyal subjects of the Monarch; the national anthem is "God Save the Queen."
· In short, no act of government which is not attributed to the Monarch's name and person.
· Her Majesty's powers, in terms of law, are uncontrolled, unrestricted and absolute.


In Practice


· The Monarch does nothing by doing everything.
· The Revolution of 1688 finally settled that in the last resort the King must give way to Parliament.
· Since then, the whole development has been marked by a steady transfer of powers and prerogatives from the Monarch as a person to the Crown as an institution.
· The King has now long ceased to be a directing factor in government and virtually performs no official act on his own initiative.
· If the King were to exercise any of the powers he exercised in the past (which he can legally do even now), he would be signing the warrant of his own abdication.
· The real power rests with the King's duly constituted Ministers.
· His Majesty remains only a symbol of authority.
· In the language of the British Constitution: "The King can do no wrong."


Supremacy of the House of Commons


· The Ministers of the Monarch are members of Parliament and remain in office as long as Parliament wishes it.
· The real power of Parliament rests with the House of Commons, a representative House of the People.
· The responsibility of the Ministers is to the House of Commons.
· The Prime Minister and other principal Ministers belong to it.
· All this means the supremacy of the House of Commons and ultimately that of the people, for the people decide the complexion of the House at a general election.
· It is the verdict of the people which determines the government.
· No government can remain oblivious of public opinion if it is to continue in office.
· Such a government is a government by consent.
· Jennings says: "Government with us is government by opinion, and that is the only kind of 'self-government' that is possible."
· Practice thus outruns theory in Britain and she presents one of the most democratic systems of government in the world.
· The Webbs used the phrase "a crowned republic" for the British system.
· Lord Morrison succinctly observed:
  · "But it takes good countries to run monarchies and it takes good monarchs to be the heads of States. And may I add that it takes a good Republic to appreciate a good monarch."


Key Points


Aspect Theory Practice
Government Absolute Monarchy Democratic republic
Form Limited constitutional Monarchy —
Powers Monarch's powers uncontrolled, unrestricted, absolute Monarch does nothing by doing everything
Real power Vested in Monarch Rests with Ministers and House of Commons
King Source of law, fountain of justice Symbol of authority; "King can do no wrong"
Parliament Summoned/dissolved by Monarch Supremacy of House of Commons
Government By Royal pleasure By consent; government by opinion (Jennings)
Description — "Crowned republic" (Webbs)

4.4 — Sovereignty of Parliament


Notes


· The British Constitution establishes the supremacy of Parliament.
· It means that Parliament is supreme.
· It can make and unmake any kind of law and no court in the realm can question its validity.
· The authority of Parliament is transcendental and absolute.
· It embraces both:
  · the enactments of ordinary laws, and
  · the most profound changes in the government itself.
· There is no judicial review and no authority can declare that the laws made by Parliament are ultra vires.
· Even the veto power has become obsolete and the Monarch must signify his assent to all measures passed by Parliament.
· Whether Parliament is really sovereign or not is a separate question.
· So far as law, pure and simple, is concerned, it is.


Key Points


Point Explanation
Supremacy Parliament is supreme
Power Make and unmake any law
No judicial review Courts cannot question validity
Transcendental Absolute authority; covers ordinary laws and profound changes
Veto obsolete Monarch must assent
Legal sovereignty Parliament is sovereign in law

4.5 — Flexible Constitution


Notes


· There is no codified and basic constitutional law having superior sanctity to statutory law.
· The power to make and amend the constitutional law is vested in Parliament.
· No special procedure is required than that attends the enactment of an ordinary bill.
· The popular ratification of constitutional amendments, required in countries like Switzerland and Australia in the nature of referendum, is unknown in Britain.
· The Constitution of the United Kingdom is flexible and responsive.
· It carries with it the advantage of centring public opinion according to the needs of the time.
· There is a facility of reform, an adaptability superior to written and more rigid constitutions.
· James Callaghan, former Labour Prime Minister, expressed the opinion that constitutions:
  · "should not be lightly tampered with, but neither should they be rigid and inflexible. They must adjust to meet the real aspirations of a nation."


Legal Controversy — Supremacy of Parliament


· The supremacy of Parliament and the ordinary easy method of changing constitutional law have been the subject of some legal controversy.
· It has been asserted that supremacy of Parliament is a legal fiction, for it is exercised in the spirit of responsibility.
· Responsibility in actual practice means the maintenance of majority in Parliament.
· As long as a Party can maintain its majority, it can get anything done.
· But this is really not so.
· How easy it is to make constitutional changes depends on:
  · the general nature of the political system prevailing in a country;
  · the attitude of the people towards constitutional amendments.
· Democratic principles and responsible institutions are the heritage of Englishmen and Parliament has never changed the law lightly and casually.
· There are profound psychological checks and voluntary restraints on the exercise of its legal authority.
· Ogg remarks:
  · "Parliament, after all, is composed of men who with few exceptions, are represented members of a well-ordered society, endowed with sense, and alive to their responsibility for safeguarding the country's political heritage. They live and work under the restraint of powerful traditions and will no more run riot with the Constitution if it were weighed down with guarantees designed to put it beyond their control."


Mandate Convention


· The "mandate convention" is indicative of the political temperament of the people.
· It enjoins that no far-reaching changes in the governmental system should be made until the voters have had a chance to express their opinion upon the proposals at a general election.


Examples of Mandate


· Asquith's Liberal Government went to the country with the scheme of Second Chamber reform.
· 1923: Stanley Baldwin appealed to the electorate on the issue of tariff.
· 1931: General election was held to elicit support for the National Government under Ramsay MacDonald.
· 1945: Labour Party fought general election on the issues of nationalisation and granting self-government to India and other subject countries.
· 1964: Labour Party put before the electorate its programme of re-nationalisation.
· Although the Party secured a precarious majority, Harold Wilson told the people immediately after forming the government:
  · "Having been charged with the duties of Government we intend to carry out those duties. Over the whole field of Government there will be many changes which we have been given a mandate by you to carry out. We intend to fulfil that mandate."


Conclusion on Flexibility


· Legally, the constitution of Britain is undeniably the most flexible in the world.
· But actually it is considerably less fluid than might be inferred from what the writers say.
· The flexibility of the Constitution does not depend wholly, or even largely, upon the simplicity of its amending process.


Key Points


Point Explanation
No codified constitutional law No superior sanctity to statutory law
Same procedure Constitutional law amended like ordinary bill
No referendum Unlike Switzerland and Australia
Flexible and responsive Centres public opinion; facility of reform
Callaghan Constitutions should not be rigid; must adjust to national aspirations
Legal controversy Supremacy of Parliament called a legal fiction
Reality Psychological checks, voluntary restraints; Parliament never changes law lightly
Ogg Parliament composed of responsible men under powerful traditions
Mandate convention No far-reaching changes without electoral mandate
Examples Asquith, Baldwin 1923, 1931 National Government, Labour 1945, Wilson 1964
Conclusion Legally most flexible; actually less fluid than inferred

4.6 — A Unitary Constitution


Notes


· The British Constitution is unitary and not federal like that of the United States of America or India.
· There is, of course, devolution, but all authority flows from the Central Government centred at London.
· The local areas, as they exist in Britain, derive their powers from Acts of Parliament which may be enlarged or restricted at its will.
· Parliament is constitutionally supreme, and the local government machine is merely an agent of the Central Government.


Essence of a Federation


· The essence of a federation is union and not unity.
· The powers and jurisdiction exercised by the units which compose a federation are:
  · original,
  · clearly demarcated, and
  · derived from the constitution.
· Neither the Central Government nor governments of the federating units can encroach upon each other's sphere.
· If any change is desired, it must be done by amending the constitution and the process of amendment is prescribed therein.
· This establishes the supremacy of the constitution.
· It means the distribution of powers is maintained by a constitution-amending authority which is superior to both central and local governments.


Britain — Unitary


· In Britain, Parliament is supreme and the local areas are subordinate units with such powers as it chooses to bestow.
· It can, if it so wished, abolish the whole complex structure of local government by a simple enactment.
· The existence of a unitary form of government is one of the reasons why Britain is able to manage without a written constitution.
· A written and rigid constitution are the pre-requisites of a federal polity.


Devolution Referendum


· If the devolution referendum in Scotland and Wales had been accepted, it would have moved the United Kingdom away from the highly centralized State that has characterised the British system over the past 250 years.
· The proposal envisaged setting up separate Assemblies in those areas with specified powers.


Key Points


Feature Unitary (Britain) Federal (USA/India)
Authority Flows from Central Government Derived from constitution
Local units Subordinate; powers from Parliament Original, demarcated powers
Parliament Supreme Limited by constitution
Amendment Ordinary procedure Special procedure
Local government Agent of Central Government Constitutionally protected
Written constitution Not required Pre-requisite
Devolution Possible; would move away from centralization —

4.7 — A Parliamentary Government


Notes


· The British Constitution provides for a Parliamentary form of government as distinct from the Presidential type.
· The King, who is a legal sovereign, has been deprived of all his powers and authority.
· The real functionaries are the Ministers who:
  · belong to the majority party in Parliament;
  · remain in office so long as they can retain its confidence.
· The Ministers are both the executive heads and members of Parliament and they co-ordinate the Legislative and Executive departments of government.
· Bagehot defines the Cabinet as:
  · "hyphen that joins, the buckle that binds the executive and legislative departments together."


No Disagreement Between Executive and Legislature


· There can be no disagreement between the Executive and Legislature.
· They work in agreement and the dangers involved in deadlock between the law-making, tax-granting authority and the Executive are absent.
· If ever the House of Commons votes against the Executive and defeats its policy, or if it should pass legislation not favoured by the Cabinet, one of two things would happen:
  1. The Cabinet must resign and enable the Opposition to form government, or
  2. It should advise the King to dissolve Parliament and order new elections, thereby giving an opportunity to the electorate to approve or disapprove the action of the Cabinet.
· There can be no continued conflict of policy between the Executive and the Legislature as may happen in the United States, where there is separation between the Executive and Legislative departments.
· Greaves observes:
  · "The right to govern in Britain flows through the legislature to the Cabinet; is not separately conferred on a popularly elected Chief Executive and in a popularly elected Parliament; the right is not capable therefore of conflicting interpretation by two bodies having an equal moral claim to speak for the public. The risks of conflict or of inanition which result from such a separation of power are attested by a wide experience, whether it be the Weimar Constitution of Germany, the federal Constitution of America, or the 1848 Constitution of France."


Key Points


Point Explanation
Parliamentary government Distinct from presidential type
King Legal sovereign but deprived of powers
Real functionaries Ministers belonging to majority party
Ministers Both executive heads and MPs
Cabinet (Bagehot) Hyphen joining, buckle binding executive and legislature
No deadlock Executive and legislature work in agreement
If defeated Cabinet resigns or dissolves Parliament
No continued conflict Unlike USA (separation of powers)
Greaves Right to govern flows through legislature to Cabinet

4.8 — Two-Party System


Notes


· Parliamentary government means party government as it provides the machinery to secure a stable government under a unified command of politically homogeneous and disciplined leaders.
· The members of the government rise and fall in unison and they are individually and collectively responsible for the policy which the Cabinet initiates and carries out.
· Since Parliamentary government is a party government, without political parties Parliamentary government is impossible.
· Such a system of government, which combines responsibility with representation, functions best when there are two parties:
  · one forming the Government,
  · the other forming the Opposition.
· Two-party system enables the views of the electors to have coherent expression.
· Britain provides the classical example of the two-party system.


Historical Development


· It originated in the seventeenth century.
· For two hundred years thereafter, only two parties functioned.
· With the emergence of the Labour Party as a major political force in 1921, three parties came into the political field.
· With the collapse of the Liberal Party, there were again two parties and the government alternated between the Conservatives and Labour.
· There was a split in the Labour Party in 1981 and a Social Democratic Party came into existence.
· The Social Democrats in alliance with the Liberals gave an impressive performance in the early stages of their emergence.
· But they were not able to make a significant dent on the two major parties.
· The alliance was short-lived and the Liberals are at present unrepresented in the House of Commons.
· Many prominent Social Democrats, too, have shifted their loyalty to their parent Labour Party.
· In fact, the British Constitution has grown and evolved under the two-party system and its working tends to maintain and perpetuate it.


Key Points


Point Explanation
Party government Parliamentary government = party government
Stable government Unified command; members rise and fall in unison
Individual and collective responsibility For Cabinet policy
Two parties Government and Opposition
Coherent expression Electors' views coherently expressed
Classical example Britain
Origin Seventeenth century; two hundred years of two parties
Labour emergence 1921 — three parties
Liberal collapse Return to two parties (Conservatives and Labour)
SDP 1981 Split in Labour; alliance with Liberals; short-lived; no significant dent
Present Two-party system maintained and perpetuated

4.9 — The Rule of Law and Civil Liberties


Notes


· One of the fundamental principles of the British Constitution is the Rule of Law.
· It is based on the Common Law of the land and is the product of centuries of struggle of the people for the recognition of their inherent rights and privileges.
· In Britain, unlike the United States or India, the Constitution does not confer specific rights on citizens.
· Nor is there any Parliamentary Act which lays down the Fundamental Rights of the people.
· Yet there is maximum liberty in Britain and according to Dicey, it is due to the existence of the Rule of Law.


Meaning of the Rule of Law


· The Rule of Law has never been enacted as a Statute.
· It is implicit in the various Acts of Parliament, judicial decisions and the Common Law.
· According to Lord Hewart, the Rule of Law means:
  · "supremacy or dominance of law, as distinguished from mere arbitrariness, or from some alternative mode, which is not law, of determining or disposing of the rights of individuals."
· It is sufficient to say that when powers of government are exercised according to settled and binding rules and not arbitrarily, then the subjects of that government are living under the Rule of Law.
· Such conditions of life can be possible only when there is:
  · equality of all before the law,
  · its supremacy,
  · uniformity, and
  · universality.
· The citizens, the courts, the administrative officials are all subject to it.
· In other words, under the Rule of Law:
  · obligations may not be imposed by the State,
  · nor property interfered with,
  · nor personal liberty curtailed,
  · except in accordance with the accepted principles of law and through the action of legally competent authorities.
· These principles are recognised by the courts and as a result judiciary is the unfailing guardian of the liberties of the people in Britain, though there is no Charter of rights to guarantee them.


Sovereignty of Parliament and Rule of Law


· Sovereignty of Parliament and the Rule of Law are closely connected.
· By its sovereign power, Parliament can curtail or suspend the liberties of the people and set aside the Rule of Law itself.
· Parliament has very often done it, but always at times of national emergency.
· Drastic restrictions were imposed upon commonly recognised rights during World War I.
· In 1934 and 1936, Incitement to Disaffection Act and Public Order Act were passed which imposed stringent restrictions on the rights to speech, assembly and press.
· Throughout World War II, drastic restraints were imposed under Emergency Powers (Defence) Act, 1939.
· But traditions of the country and political temperament of the people do not tolerate such infringement of their liberties when conditions of national emergency or danger are not prevailing.
· There is a sense in which Parliament itself is subject to the Rule of Law.
· It cannot, and in fact it does not, make laws which unnecessarily encroach upon the liberties of the people.
· Laws in Britain are passed to promote liberty and not to restrict liberty.
· "Freedom of speech is as truly a part of the British way of life as the responsibility of ministers. Neither rests upon written law; neither would be observed more consistently if it did so."


Key Points


Point Explanation
Fundamental principle Rule of Law
Based on Common Law; centuries of struggle
No specific rights Unlike USA/India; no Fundamental Rights Act
Maximum liberty Due to Rule of Law (Dicey)
Not enacted Implicit in Acts, judicial decisions, Common Law
Lord Hewart Supremacy/dominance of law vs. arbitrariness
Conditions Equality before law; supremacy; uniformity; universality
Subject to it Citizens, courts, administrative officials
Protection Judiciary as unfailing guardian; no Charter of rights
Parliament's power Can curtail liberties; done at times of national emergency
Examples WWI restrictions; 1934/1936 Acts; WWII Emergency Powers Act 1939
Limits Traditions and political temperament do not tolerate infringement when no emergency
Purpose Laws passed to promote liberty, not restrict it

4.10 — Hereditary Character


Notes


· Another special distinctive feature of the British Constitution is the recognition given to the hereditary principle, which has been, for so long, discarded by the great majority of other countries.
· Monarchy rests on the hereditary principle.
· The House of Lords is primarily composed of hereditary peers.
· It is true that neither the King nor the House of Lords play any effective role in the political set-up of the country.
· Yet their continuance appears hardly reconcilable with the democratic ideals which Englishmen cherish so fondly.
· And still Englishmen had never been in a mood to abolish these historic institutions.
· Attlee observed:
  · "I would claim that, despite the maintenance of monarchical and oligarchical elements, the British system is the best example of democracy and especially of parliamentary democracy."


Key Points


Point Explanation
Hereditary principle Recognised in British Constitution
Discarded elsewhere Great majority of other countries have discarded it
Monarchy Rests on hereditary principle
House of Lords Primarily hereditary peers
Role Neither King nor Lords play effective role
Paradox Hardly reconcilable with democratic ideals, yet not abolished
Attlee Despite monarchical and oligarchical elements, best example of democracy
