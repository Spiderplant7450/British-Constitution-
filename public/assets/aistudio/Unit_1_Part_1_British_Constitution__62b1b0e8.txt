Topic 1.1 — Meaning of “Constitution”: Written vs. Unwritten


1.1.1 General Meaning of “Constitution”


· In almost every country except the United Kingdom, the term “Constitution” means:
  · a selection of legal rules
  · which delineate the government of that country
  · and which are embodied in one or several documents.
· Such a document may be drawn up by:
  · a Constituent Assembly, or
  · a legislature, or
  · granted by a King, binding himself and his successors to govern according to its provisions.
· Thus, in the ordinary sense, a Constitution means:
  · a written, precise and systematic document
  · containing the general principles under which government functions.


1.1.2 Characteristics of a Written Constitution


· It is distinct in character.
· It is the supreme law of the land.
· It is held in special sanctity.
· It is amended by a special procedure, different from the procedure for amending ordinary/statutory law.
· Ordinary/statutory law must be consistent with:
  · the letter of the Constitution, and
  · the spirit of the Constitution.
· If statutory law is inconsistent, it is held:
  · unconstitutional, or
  · ultra vires (beyond legal power),
  · as soon as a court gets an opportunity to review it.


1.1.3 The British Departure from the General Meaning


· The British Constitution has never been devised and reduced to writing.
· It remains:
  · undefined,
  · unsystematised, and
  · undecided.
· It lacks:
  · precision, and
  · coherence.
· Englishmen never drew out their political system in the shape of a formal document.
· Therefore, there is no single place where “The Constitution” as a whole is clearly and definitely written down.
· Many books describe the British Constitution, but no single book can be said to contain it.


1.1.4 Scattered Nature of British Constitutional Rules


· Some enactments of Parliament make up the British Constitution, but:
  · they do not bear the same date;
  · they are scattered;
  · they were made as and when needed, according to circumstances.
· The most important part of the British Constitution is:
  · what is kept out of written law, and
  · given over to the sole guardianship of custom.
· There is no law in the UK which can be called constitutional merely because it can be altered by a different procedure from ordinary statutory law.


1.1.5 Constitutional Law and Statutory Law in Britain


· In Britain, Constitutional Law and Statutory/Ordinary Law stand at par with one another.
· Both:
  · emanate from the same source;
  · undergo the same procedure in passing and amending.
· Therefore:
  · no court or any other authority can legally refuse to enforce or set aside an enactment of Parliament.


1.1.6 British Constitution as Unwritten, Flexible and Evolutionary


· The British Constitution is, to a large extent:
  · an unwritten Constitution, and
  · a flexible Constitution.
· It is:
  · the product of history;
  · the result of evolution;
  · grown with the growth of the English nation;
  · changed with its wants;
  · adapted to the needs of various times.
· Jennings’ observation:
  · “If the Constitution consists of institutions and not of the paper that describes them, the British Constitution has not been made but has grown—and there is no paper.”
· Institutions were established from time to time as need arose.
· They were formed to meet immediate requirements, then adapted to broader and sometimes different functions.
· There has been a constant process of:
  · invention,
  · reform, and
  · amended distribution of powers.
· The building has been constantly:
  · added to,
  · patched, and
  · partly reconstructed,
  · but never razed and rebuilt on a new foundation.


1.1.7 British Constitution as a Body of Basic Rules


· Briefly, the British Constitution is:
  · a body of basic rules
  · indicating the structure and functions of political institutions
  · and the principles governing their operation.
· It is the same in nature as the constitution of any other country.
· The only difference is that the British Constitution has never been systematised, codified and put in orderly form.
· Probably no future attempt will be made to bring all rules and principles together into a consistent and coherent whole.
· This is practically impossible because:
  · usages and traditions cover a wide range;
  · many of them are not sufficiently definite to be reduced to writing.
· Moreover, the Englishman as a political entity has never favoured a government based on fixed principles involving exact rules.
· He is:
  · practical,
  · matter-of-fact,
  · zealous for business.
· Expediency is his guiding principle.
· The British Constitution therefore lacks logic, but it is not a mere hotchpotch of heterogeneous elements.
· Its rules and principles have been deduced from British experience and consciously adhered to and applied.


1.1.8 Paine and Tocqueville: “The British Constitution Does Not Exist”


· Thomas Paine:
  · a champion of written constitutions;
  · declared that where a Constitution “cannot be produced in a visible form, there is none.”
  · asked Burke: “Can Mr. Burke produce the English Constitution?”
  · concluded that no such thing as a Constitution exists or ever did exist.
· Alexis de Tocqueville:
  · said that in England the Constitution may go on changing continually, or rather it does not exist.
· Rebuttal:
  · Paine and Tocqueville were wrong.
  · There can be no State without a constitution.
  · It is true that Britain has no single document comprising fundamental constitutional rules.
  · But no constitution is either wholly written or entirely unwritten.
  · Written and unwritten elements are present in every constitution.


1.1.9 Written and Unwritten Elements in Every Constitution


· All written constitutions grow and expand with time through:
  · customs, and
  · judicial interpretations.
· Bryce’s observation:
  · Written constitutions become “developed by interpretation, fringed with decisions, and enlarged by customs,” so that after a time the letter of the text no longer conveys their full effect.
· Makers of a written constitution cannot foresee the future.
· Man is dynamic, and so are his political institutions.
· The conventional element in any system of government is inevitable.
· A written constitution does not contain all rules relating to all institutions of government.
· A selection is made.
· Examples:
  · US Constitution: only seven Articles, about ten pages.
  · Indian Constitution: lengthiest in the world, 395 Articles and twelve Schedules.
· This shows that a written constitution may contain as much or as little as the framers think desirable.
· Yet no constitution is complete by itself:
  · it is a framework, a skeleton;
  · it must be filled out with detailed rules and practices;
  · it deals with principal institutions, main functions, and important rights and duties;
  · it may contain more or less according to circumstances and problems at the time of drafting.
· All written constitutions provide for amendments.
· Customs and judicial decisions also supplement constitutional provisions.
· Therefore, the difference between a written and an unwritten constitution is:
  · one of degree, not of kind.


1.1.10 Key Conclusion


· Wherever there are rules determining the creation and operation of governmental institutions, there exists a constitution.
· Britain has such institutions and such rules.
· Long before Paine and Tocqueville, England had such a body of rules, with Englishmen equally conscious of its existence and proud of its history.

Topic 1.2 — British Constitution as Undefined, Unsystematised, and Largely Unwritten


1.2.1 Undefined and Unsystematised Character


· The British Constitution has never been devised and reduced to writing.
· It remains:
  · undefined,
  · unsystematised, and
  · undecided.
· It lacks:
  · precision, and
  · coherence.


1.2.2 No Single Document / No Codification


· Englishmen never drew out their political system in the shape of a formal document.
· Consequently, there is no single place in which “The Constitution” as a whole is clearly and definitely written down.
· Many books describe the British Constitution, but no one of them can be said to contain it.


1.2.3 Scattered Enactments


· Some enactments of Parliament make the British Constitution, but:
  · they do not bear the same date;
  · they are scattered;
  · they were made as and when they were needed and the circumstances demanded.


1.2.4 Custom as the Most Important Part


· The most important part of the British Constitution is:
  · just what is kept out of the written law, and
  · given over to the sole guardianship of custom.


1.2.5 Constitutional Law and Statutory Law at Par


· There is no law in the UK of which we can say:
  · “since it is a part of the Constitution, it can be altered by a procedure different from the one required for altering the statutory law.”
· Here, Constitutional Law and Statutory/Ordinary Law:
  · stand at par with one another;
  · emanate from the same source;
  · undergo the same procedure in passing and amending.


1.2.6 No Judicial Review of Parliament’s Enactments


· Obviously, then:
  · no court or any other authority can legally refuse to enforce or set aside any enactment of Parliament.


1.2.7 Unwritten and Flexible Constitution


· The British Constitution is, therefore, to a large extent:
  · an unwritten Constitution, and
  · a flexible Constitution.


1.2.8 Product of History and Evolution


· It is:
  · the product of history;
  · the result of evolution;
  · grown with the growth of the English nation;
  · changed with its wants;
  · adapted itself to the needs of various times.


1.2.9 Jennings’ Observation


· Jennings aptly remarked:
  · “If the Constitution consists of institutions and not of the paper that describes them, the British Constitution has not been made but has grown—and there is no paper.”
· Institutions necessary for carrying out State functions were established from time to time as the need arose.
· They were:
  · formed to meet immediate requirements;
  · then adapted to exercise more extensive and sometimes different functions.
· From time to time, political and economic circumstances have called for reforms.
· There has been a constant process of:
  · invention,
  · reform, and
  · amended distribution of powers.
· The building has been constantly:
  · added to,
  · patched, and
  · partly reconstructed,
  · so that it has been renewed from century to century,
  · but it has never been razed and rebuilt on new foundation.


1.2.10 “Child of Wisdom and Chance”


· The British Constitution is “the child of wisdom and chance”.
· It is the result of a process in which many elements have entered, piling themselves one upon another from age to age:
  · charters,
  · statutes,
  · judicial decisions,
  · precedents,
  · usages, and
  · traditions.
· These have shaped the political institutions of the country according to the exigencies of time.


1.2.11 Ever-Growing and Dynamic


· The British Constitution is:
  · ever growing;
  · always undergoing modifications;
  · a dynamic Constitution with its roots in the past and branches in the future.
· Lord Morrison cogently said:
  · “But as a whole, ours has been a peaceful development, learning as we moved on, establishing the foundation of further progress.”
· No man in 1688 could have foretold with accuracy what the Constitution of present-day Britain would be.
· No man of our times can predict how the Constitution will evolve a few decades hence.


1.2.12 Summary of the Nature


· Briefly, the British Constitution is:
  · a body of basic rules;
  · indicating the structure and functions of political institutions;
  · and the principles governing their operation.
· It is the same in nature as the constitution of any other country.
· The only difference is that the British Constitution has never been systematised, codified and put in an orderly form.


1.2.13 No Future Codification Likely


· Probably, no attempt will be made in future to bring all these rules and principles together to make the Constitution a consistent and coherent whole.
· In fact, it is an impossible task, because:
  · usages and traditions cover a wide range;
  · many of them are not sufficiently definite to be reduced to writing.
· Moreover, the Englishman, as a political entity, has never favoured a system of government based upon fixed principles involving the application of exact rules.
· He is:
  · practical,
  · matter-of-fact, and
  · zealous for business.
· Expediency is the guiding principle of his life, and he seizes opportunity by the forelock.
· He knows no logic, and the British Constitution lacks all logic.
· Ogg says:
  · “the result is a constitutional structure which lacks symmetry, governmental system which abounds in the illogical.”
· But this does not mean it is a mere hotchpotch of heterogeneous elements.
· The rules and principles governing the governmental machinery have been:
  · deduced from British experience, and
  · consciously adhered to and applied.

Topic 1.3 — Historical Growth: Product of History and Evolution


1.3.1 Core Idea


· The British Constitution is not a pre-arranged pattern according to which government must be carried on.
· It is not the result of conscious creation.
· Its sources are several, and the course of its development has been:
  · sometimes guided by accident,
  · sometimes by high design.


1.3.2 “Child of Wisdom and Chance”


· It is a “child of wisdom and of chance”.
· Its growth has been:
  · piecemeal, and
  · gradual.
· It has expressed itself in different Charters and Statutes, precedents, usages and traditions—whatever the exigencies of time demanded.


1.3.3 No Single Document


· Accordingly, there is no single document which can be said to contain the general principles of political government.
· In fact, no attempt has ever been made to embody these principles in a documentary form.
· They remain scattered.
· Some rules and principles have been reduced to writing and embodied in Acts of Parliament, but a greater part still remains unwritten and is:
  · “simply carried in men’s minds as precedents, decisions, habits, and practices.”
· The written elements taken by themselves do not comprise the Constitution, though they have considerably affected it.


1.3.4 Growth Like an Organism


· The British Constitution has grown like an organism and developed from age to age.
· It fulfils Sir James McIntosh’s dictum that constitutions grow instead of being made.
· It is the product of evolution and the result of slow and steady development and successive accretions spreading over a thousand years.


1.3.5 No Revolutionary Political Upheavals


· All through this period, Britain has never witnessed political upheavals of a revolutionary character.
· In fact, all political revolutions, if they may be described as revolutions, have been of conservative nature.
· Britain has all through moved along an essentially continuous constitutional pathway, readjusting her institutions slowly and cautiously to the changing conditions and needs of the country and its people.


1.3.6 Ogg’s Observation


· Ogg says:
  · political changes “have as a rule been so gradual,
  · deference to traditions so habitual,
  · and the disposition to cling to accustomed names and forms even when the spirit has changed, so deep-seated,
  · that the constitutional history of Britain displays a continuity hardly paralleled in any other land.”


1.3.7 Lord Morrison’s View


· Lord Morrison is of the opinion that such a peaceful evolution of the British Constitution is:
  · “more permanent,
  · more satisfactory, and
  · less painful,
  · than if it were the result of the violent bloody revolution,”
· and he thinks that his “country has been lucky in that respect.”


1.3.8 Freeman’s Observation (from Section 4 Development of the Constitution — relevant here)


· Freeman emphasised the feature of continuous development with unquestionable accuracy:
  · “The continual national life of the people, notwithstanding foreign conquests and internal revolutions, has remained unbroken for fourteen hundred years.
  · At no moment has the tie between the present and the past been wholly rent asunder;
  · at no moment have Englishmen sat down to put together a wholly new constitution in obedience to some dazzling theory.
  · Each step in our growth has been the natural consequence of some earlier step;
  · each change in our land and constitution has been, not the beginning in anything wholly new, but the development and improvement of something that was already old.
  · Our progress has in some ages been faster, in others slower; at some moments we have seemed to stand still, or even to go back, but the great mark of political development has never wholly stopped;
  · it has never been permanently checked since the days when the coming in of the Teutonic conquerors first began to change Britain into England.”


1.3.9 Starting Point


· The starting point of the British Constitution and the principles which govern their working lie scattered into the past.
· The present mechanism of government can only be understood if we analyse the process of this growth:
  · how the British Constitution came into being, and
  · how it assumed its present form and stature.


1.3.10 Periods of Growth (brief mention — detailed in Topic 5)


· It is customary to divide this process of growth into six distinct periods, but the text divides them into three as a matter of practical utility:
  1. First period: Angles and Saxons through Norman and Angevin dynasties to 1485 — foundations of the Constitution.
  2. Second period: 1485 to 1689 — Tudor dynasty through early and later Stuart periods, including Puritan Revolution and Commonwealth — reconstruction of the Constitution.
  3. Third period: 1689 to the present — rounding out / fructification of the Constitution.
· By the end of the fifteenth century:
  · Parliament had begun to show marks of its strength;
  · the King’s power had definitely eclipsed;
  · the great institutional foundation of the modern English Constitution had been firmly laid.
· The years to come were in the nature of further growth and adjustment of these institutions, leading to altered balances of power and mechanisms of control.

Topic 1.4 — Nature of the British Constitution: Body of Basic Rules; Dynamic; Practical; Lacks Logic/Symmetry


1.4.1 Definition — A Body of Basic Rules


· Briefly, the British Constitution is:
  · a body of basic rules
  · indicating the structure and functions of political institutions
  · and the principles governing their operation.
· It is just the same in nature as the constitution of any other country.
· The only difference is that the British Constitution has never been systematised, codified and put in an orderly form.


1.4.2 Same in Nature as Other Constitutions


· The British Constitution is not different in kind from other constitutions.
· It performs the same function: laying down basic rules about institutions and their working.
· But it remains undefined, unsystematised and undecided.
· It lacks precision and coherence.
· No single document contains it.


1.4.3 Dynamic Nature


· The British Constitution is ever growing and always undergoing modifications.
· It is a dynamic Constitution with its roots in the past and branches in the future.
· It has grown with the growth of the English nation, changed with its wants, and adapted itself to the needs of various times.
· No man in 1688 could have foretold the present Constitution, and no man today can predict how it will evolve decades hence.


1.4.4 Practical and Expedient Character


· The Englishman, as a political entity, has never favoured a system of government based upon fixed principles involving the application of exact rules.
· He is:
  · practical,
  · matter-of-fact, and
  · zealous for business.
· Expediency is the guiding principle of his life.
· He seizes opportunity by the forelock.
· The Constitution is shaped by practical needs rather than abstract theory.


1.4.5 Lacks Logic


· The Englishman knows no logic, and the British Constitution lacks all logic.
· It has not been built on a single logical plan or grand theory.
· It has grown piecemeal, by accident and design, as circumstances demanded.
· There is no attempt to make it a consistent and coherent whole.


1.4.6 Ogg’s Observation — Lacks Symmetry, Abounds in the Illogical


· Ogg says:
  · “the result is a constitutional structure which lacks symmetry, governmental system which abounds in the illogical.”
· This means the Constitution is not neatly arranged or logically symmetrical.
· It contains many survivals, anomalies and illogical features.


1.4.7 Not a Mere Hotchpotch


· But this does not mean that it is a mere hotchpotch of heterogeneous elements.
· The rules and principles which govern the governmental machinery have been:
  · deduced from British experience, and
  · consciously adhered to and applied.
· So there is order and purpose, even if not logical symmetry.


1.4.8 Anson’s Summary of the Nature


· Anson summed up the nature of the British Constitution:
  · It is “a somewhat rambling structure,
  · and like a house which many successive owners have altered just so far as suited their immediate wants or fashion of the time,
  · it bears the marks of many hands,
  · and is convenient rather than symmetrical.
  · Forms and phrases survive which have long since lost their meaning,
  · and the adaptation of practice to convenience by a process of unconscious change has brought about in many cases a divergence of law and custom, of theory and practice.”


1.4.9 Bagehot’s Prerequisites


· Walter Bagehot in The English Constitution asserted that such a system of government as obtainable in Britain was possible because there existed certain prerequisites:
  · mutual confidence among electors,
  · a calm national mind, and
  · the gift of rationality.
· All these qualities add up to an adult and practical nation.
· The obvious result is that “the British for the most part think that the nature of their Constitution is most sensible and that a codified constitution like the American is more trouble than it is worth.”


1.4.10 Summary Table of Nature


Feature Explanation
Body of basic rules Structure/functions of institutions and principles of operation
Same in nature as others Only difference: not systematised/codified
Dynamic Ever-growing; roots in past, branches in future
Practical Expediency, not abstract theory; matter-of-fact
Lacks logic No single logical plan; grown piecemeal
Lacks symmetry (Ogg) Illogical features; anomalies survive
Not a hotchpotch Rules deduced from experience and consciously applied

Topic 1.5 — Debate: Did the British Constitution Exist? (Paine and Tocqueville vs. Rebuttal)


1.5.1 The Assertion


· Thomas Paine and Alexis de Tocqueville were prominent among many writers who held the opinion that the British Constitution did not exist.
· They argued that since Britain had no single written document, there was no constitution properly so called.
· The text firmly concludes that both were wrong.


1.5.2 Thomas Paine’s Argument


· Paine was a great champion of written constitutions.
· He categorically declared:
  · Where a Constitution “cannot be produced in a visible form, there is none.”
· In a spirited reply to Burke (who defended the British Constitution in Reflections on the French Revolution), Paine asked:
  · “Can Mr. Burke produce the English Constitution?”
· Paine concluded:
  · If he cannot, we may fairly conclude that no such thing as a Constitution exists or ever did exist, though it has been so much talked about.


1.5.3 Tocqueville’s View


· De Tocqueville, a celebrated French writer on foreign governments, said a generation later:
  · In “England the Constitution may go on changing continually or rather it does not exist.”
· Like Paine, he equated the absence of a single written document with the absence of a constitution.


1.5.4 The Rebuttal — Why Paine and Tocqueville Were Wrong


(a) No State Without a Constitution


· There can be no State without a constitution.
· Every state must have some body of rules governing its institutions and their operation.
· Britain has such institutions and such rules.


(b) No Constitution Is Wholly Written or Entirely Unwritten


· It is true that Britain has no single document comprising fundamental constitutional rules (unlike the USA or India).
· But no constitution is either wholly written or entirely unwritten.
· Written and unwritten elements are present in every constitution.


(c) Written Constitutions Grow and Expand


· All written constitutions grow and expand with the passage of time through:
  · customs, and
  · judicial interpretations.
· Bryce’s observation:
  · Written constitutions become “developed by interpretation, fringed with decisions, and enlarged by customs”, so that after a time the letter of their texts no longer conveys their full effect.


(d) Makers Cannot Foresee the Future


· The makers of a written constitution cannot foresee the future.
· They cannot shape the constitution to fulfill the needs of people to come.
· Man is dynamic, and so are his political institutions.
· The conventional element in any system of government is inevitable.


(e) A Written Constitution Does Not Contain All Rules


· A written constitution does not contain all rules relating to all institutions of government.
· A selection is made of both (rules and institutions).
· Examples:
  · US Constitution: only seven Articles, about ten pages.
  · Indian Constitution: lengthiest in the world, 395 Articles and twelve Schedules.
· This shows that a written constitution may contain as much or as little as the framers think desirable.


(f) No Constitution Is Complete by Itself


· No constitution is complete by itself.
· It is a framework, a skeleton which has to be filled out with detailed rules and practices.
· It deals with:
  · principal institutions and their main functions;
  · rights and duties of citizens regarded as important at the time.
· It may contain more or less, according to circumstances and problems faced at the time of drafting.


(g) Amendments and Customs Supplement Constitutional Provisions


· All written constitutions provide for amendments to cater to future needs.
· Customs and judicial decisions also supplement constitutional provisions.


(h) Difference Is One of Degree, Not Kind


· The difference between a written and an unwritten constitution is:
  · one of degree, not of kind.
· Wherever there are rules determining the creation and operation of governmental institutions, there exists a constitution.


1.5.5 Conclusion


· Britain has such institutions and such rules.
· Long before the times of Paine and Tocqueville, England had such a body of rules, with Englishmen equally conscious of its existence and proud of its history.
· Therefore, the British Constitution did and does exist, even though it is not codified in a single document.

Topic 1.6 — Written vs. Unwritten Constitutions: Difference of Degree, Not Kind; No Constitution Complete by Itself


1.6.1 The Core Distinction


· The difference between a written and an unwritten constitution is:
  · one of degree, not of kind.
· This means:
  · it is a matter of how much is written down, not whether a constitution exists.
· Wherever there are rules determining the creation and operation of governmental institutions, there exists a constitution.
· Britain has such institutions and such rules, therefore Britain has a constitution.


1.6.2 No Constitution Is Wholly Written or Entirely Unwritten


· No constitution is either wholly written or entirely unwritten.
· Written and unwritten elements are present in every constitution.
· Even the most detailed written constitution relies on:
  · customs,
  · conventions, and
  · judicial interpretations.
· Even the most unwritten constitution (like Britain’s) contains:
  · statutes,
  · judicial decisions, and
  · documentary sources.


1.6.3 Written Constitutions Grow and Expand (Bryce’s Observation)


· All written constitutions grow and expand with the passage of time.
· This growth occurs through:
  · customs, and
  · judicial interpretations.
· Bryce remarked that written constitutions become:
  · **“developed by interpretation,
  · fringed with decisions,
  · and enlarged by customs,
  · so that after a time the letter of their texts no longer conveys their full effect.”**
· This shows that even written constitutions are not static; they evolve like unwritten ones.


1.6.4 Makers Cannot Foresee the Future


· The makers of a written constitution cannot foresee the future.
· They cannot shape the constitution to fulfill the needs of people to come.
· Man is dynamic, and so are his political institutions.
· Therefore, the conventional element in any system of government is inevitable.
· No written constitution can provide for every future circumstance.


1.6.5 A Written Constitution Does Not Contain All Rules


· A written constitution does not contain all rules relating to all institutions of government.
· A selection is made of both:
  · rules, and
  · institutions.
· Examples:
  · US Constitution: only seven Articles, occupies about ten pages.
  · Indian Constitution: lengthiest in the world, 395 Articles and twelve Schedules.
· The difference between the two is suggestive:
  · it shows that within limits, a written constitution may contain as much or as little as is thought desirable by the framers.


1.6.6 No Constitution Is Complete by Itself


· No constitution is complete by itself.
· It is:
  · a framework,
  · a skeleton,
  · which has to be filled out with detailed rules and practices.
· A constitution is concerned with:
  · the principal institutions and their main functions;
  · the rights and duties of citizens which are, for the time being, regarded as important.
· It may contain more or less, according to:
  · the circumstances of the moment, and
  · the special problems being faced by the State while it is being drafted.


1.6.7 Amendments and Supplementary Sources


· All written constitutions provide for amendments in order to cater to the future needs of the people.
· Customs and judicial decisions also supplement the constitutional provisions.
· Therefore, even a written constitution is:
  · incomplete without conventions,
  · incomplete without judicial interpretation, and
  · incomplete without customs.


1.6.8 Conclusion — Degree, Not Kind


· The difference between written and unwritten constitutions is therefore:
  · one of degree, not of kind.
· Every constitution is a mixture of written and unwritten elements.
· The British Constitution is not unique in being partly unwritten; it is only more unwritten than most.
· Conversely, written constitutions are not wholly written; they are only more written than Britain’s.
· Wherever there are rules determining the creation and operation of governmental institutions, there exists a constitution.
· Britain has such institutions and such rules; long before Paine and Tocqueville, England had such a body of rules, with Englishmen conscious of its existence and proud of its history.
