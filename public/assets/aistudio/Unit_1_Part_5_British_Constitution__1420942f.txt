Topic 5 — Development of the Constitution


5.1 Introduction — Continuous National Development (Freeman's View)


Notes


· Freeman emphasised the feature of continuous development with unquestionable accuracy.
· His famous observation:
  · "The continual national life of the people, notwithstanding foreign conquests and internal revolutions, has remained unbroken for fourteen hundred years.
  · At no moment has the tie between the present and the past been wholly rent asunder;
  · at no moment have Englishmen sat down to put together a wholly new constitution in obedience to some dazzling theory.
  · Each step in our growth has been the natural consequence of some earlier step;
  · each change in our land and constitution has been, not the beginning in anything wholly new, but the development and improvement of something that was already old.
  · Our progress has in some ages been faster, in others slower;
  · at some moments we have seemed to stand still, or even to go back,
  · but the great mark of political development has never wholly stopped;
  · it has never been permanently checked since the days when the coming in of the Teutonic conquerors first began to change Britain into England."


Key Points


Point Explanation
Freeman Continuous development; unbroken for 1400 years
No wholly new constitution Englishmen never sat down to make a wholly new constitution
Each step natural Natural consequence of some earlier step
Development of old Each change is development of something already old
Never wholly stopped Progress faster/slower, but never permanently checked
Starting point Teutonic conquerors changing Britain into England

5.2 Starting Point and Need for Historical Analysis


Notes


· The starting point of the British Constitution and the principles which govern their working lie scattered into the past.
· The present mechanism of government can only be understood if we analyse the process of this growth:
  · how the British Constitution came into being;
  · how it assumed its present form and stature.


Key Points


Point Explanation
Starting point Scattered into the past
Understanding Requires analysis of process of growth
Two questions How it came into being; how it assumed present form

5.3 The Three Periods of Growth


Notes


· It is customary to divide this process of growth into six distinct periods.
· But the text divides them into three as a matter of practical utility.


The Three Periods


Period Duration Name Description
First Angles and Saxons → Norman and Angevin dynasties → 1485 Foundations of the Constitution Laid the foundations
Second 1485 to 1689 Reconstruction of the Constitution Tudor dynasty through early and later Stuart periods; Puritan Revolution and Commonwealth
Third 1689 to present Rounding out / Fructification of the Constitution Direct interest to students of British Government today


Key Points


Point Explanation

5.4 End of the Fifteenth Century — Position


Notes


· By the end of the fifteenth century:
  · Parliament had begun to show marks of its strength;
  · the King's power had definitely eclipsed;
  · the great institutional foundation of the modern English Constitution had been firmly laid.
· The years to come were in the nature of:
  · further growth and adjustment of these institutions;
  · leading to altered balances of power and mechanisms of control.


Key Points


Point Explanation
Parliament Begun to show marks of strength
King's power Definitely eclipsed
Institutional foundation Firmly laid
Future years Further growth and adjustment; altered balances of power


---


5.5 The Third Period — Glorious Revolution of 1689


Notes


· The third period extends from 1689 to the present.
· It is of more direct interest to students of British Government today.
· In this period came the rounding out, or fructification of the Constitution.
· The Glorious Revolution of 1689 drew to a close the great constitutional struggle of the seventeenth century.
· Kings in future held the throne by the grace of Parliament.
· Kings could be made and unmade by Parliament.
· Parliament was, therefore, Supreme.
· The Bill of Rights embodied the constitutional rules and principles which should guide the transactions of the King in his dealings with Parliament.
· It stated clearly and definitely the limitations on the powers of the King.
· In one specific clause, it decreed that no future ruler of England could be a Roman Catholic or could marry a Roman Catholic.


Key Points


Point Explanation
Glorious Revolution 1689 — closed great constitutional struggle of 17th century
Kings Held throne by grace of Parliament; could be made/unmade by Parliament
Parliament Supreme
Bill of Rights Embodied constitutional rules; limitations on King's powers
Religious clause No future ruler could be Roman Catholic or marry a Roman Catholic


---


5.6 Bill of Rights — Culminating Point


Notes


· The Bill of Rights marked the culminating point in the evolution of the fundamentals.
· The centre of gravity had shifted from the King to Parliament.
· But it was many years before the change became clearly understood.
· It took time for seeds sown in earlier periods to:
  · germinate,
  · grow,
  · and become fully matured institutions of popular government.


Key Points


Point Explanation
Bill of Rights Culminating point in evolution of fundamentals
Shift Centre of gravity from King to Parliament
Time lag Many years before change understood
Seeds Sown in earlier periods; took time to germinate and mature


---


5.7 Main Lines of Growth and Development


Notes


Following are the main lines of growth and development which complete our account in making the British Constitution what it stands for today:


1. Diminished powers of the King
2. Emergence of the Cabinet and consequently responsible Ministry
3. Rise of political parties
4. Leadership of the Minister (Prime Minister)
5. Shifting of power within Parliament
6. Democratisation of the House of Commons as a result of enactment of a series of Reform Bills beginning from 1832
7. Great constitutional changes which altered the character of the British Empire
8. Latest change made by the Labour Government — reduced the absolute number as well as the hereditary element in the composition of the House of Lords


Key Points


# Line of Development Explanation
1 Diminished powers of the King Gradual transfer of power
2 Emergence of Cabinet Responsible Ministry
3 Rise of political parties Party system
4 Leadership of the Minister Prime Minister's leadership
5 Shifting of power within Parliament Commons supremacy
6 Democratisation of House of Commons Reform Bills from 1832
7 Changes in Empire Altered character of British Empire
8 House of Lords reform Labour Government reduced absolute number and hereditary element
