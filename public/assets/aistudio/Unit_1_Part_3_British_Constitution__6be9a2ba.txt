Topic 3 — Conventions of the Constitution (Most Important)


Topic 3.1 & 3.2 — Meaning, Place, and Distinction from Laws


3.1 Meaning and Place of Conventions


3.1.1 What Are Conventions?


· Dicey gave the name “Conventions of the Constitution” to the indefinite number of customs, traditions and precedents.
· They form an integral part of the British Constitution.
· They are not the law of the Constitution.
· They are nowhere written down in any formal or official document.


3.1.2 Deep-Rooted in English Habits


· Conventions are so deep-rooted in the habits of Englishmen.
· The mechanism of government is so firmly erected on their foundation that:
  · without them the Constitution becomes maimed, if not absolutely unworkable.


3.1.3 Place of Conventions


· Conventions are the centre and soul of the constitutional law in Britain.
· They cover some of the most important parts of the British political system.
· They are observed with due respect, even though they cannot be enforced by courts.


3.1.4 Fundamental Convention


· The fundamental convention, from which practically all others flow, is the convention of Cabinet Government.


---


3.2 Distinction Between Laws of the Constitution and Conventions


3.2.1 The Traditional Distinction


· A distinction is very often made between:
  · laws of the Constitution, and
  · conventions of the Constitution.


3.2.2 But Conventions Are Not Really Very Different from Laws


· Conventions are not really very different from laws.
· It is frequently difficult to place a set of rules in one class or the other.


3.2.3 Jennings’ View — General Acquiescence


· Jennings has rightly said:
  · Conventions, like most fundamental rules of any Constitution, rest essentially upon general acquiescence.
  · “A written constitution is not law because somebody has made it, but it has been accepted.”
· Conventions are based on usage and acquiescence.
· Their binding force, like laws, is derived from the willingness of the people to be so bound.


3.2.4 Obedience to Law vs. Obedience to Conventions


· If obedience to law is deemed a fundamental duty,
· obedience to conventions is among the political obligations.
· Because conventions help the wheels of political machine going in accordance with the will of the people.


3.2.5 Similarities Between Law and Conventions


· Both serve the common purpose of:
  · regulating the structure and functions of government;
  · aiming at the good of the people.
· Both are the result of common consent.
· Both are inevitably similar.


3.2.6 Technical Differences — Three Aspects


Aspect Law Convention
1. Origin Emanates from a legally constituted body; carries greater sanctity Extra-legal; grows out of practice; existence determined by usage
2. Formulation Usually expressed in more precise terms; has the added dignity of extracting unquestioning obedience from everybody Never formulated; grows out of practice; modified by practice; at any given time it may be difficult to say whether a practice has become a convention
3. Enforcement Enforced by the courts; judges consider whether Acts are legally valid and take steps to ensure obedience Not enforced by the courts; judges cannot force obedience; no legal sanction


3.2.7 No Definite Boundary Line


· Even from the technical point of view, no definite boundary line can be drawn between:
  · legislation, on the one hand, and
  · conventions, on the other hand.
· If a given provision is part of the British Constitution, it is either law or convention.
· The fundamental conventions have well-nigh been recognised by many Acts of Parliament.


3.2.8 Examples of Recognition by Legislation


· Preamble to the British North America Act, 1867 (now Canada Act):
  · “the Provinces of... have expressed their desire to be federally united into one Dominion under the Crown of the United Kingdom... with a constitution similar in principle to that of the United Kingdom.”
· Article 2 of the Agreement for a Treaty between Great Britain and Ireland, 1921:
  · the law, practice, and constitutional usage governing the relationship of the Crown or of its representatives, or of the Imperial Parliament to the Dominion of Canada, were made applicable to the Irish Free State.
· Section 4 of the Status of the Union Act, 1934:
  · specific reference to the constitutional conventions regulating the use by the Governor-General of his legal power of summoning and dissolving Parliament, and of appointing ministers.
· Preamble to the Statute of Westminster:
  · some conventions regulating relationship between the Dominions and the United Kingdom inserted;
  · particularly those relating to alterations in the law touching the Succession to the Throne, or the Royal Style and Titles;
  · and the legislative authority of the British Parliament.
· The importance of the first of these conventions was demonstrated in the abdication of Edward VIII.
· The change in the Royal Style and Titles after the Indian Independence Act, 1947 was brought about by the full assent of the Dominion Parliament.


3.2.9 Cabinet and Prime Minister — Recognised by Legislation


· The Cabinet system presupposes the pre-eminence and leadership of one single person — the Prime Minister.
· Abolish the institution of Prime Minister or diminish any part of his powers, the entire political structure would be destroyed.
· Yet neither the institution of Cabinet nor the office of the Prime Minister were known to law before 1937.
· Ministers of the Crown Act, 1937:
  · provided for payment of £10,000 a year “to the person who is the Prime Minister and the First Lord of the Treasury”;
  · provided for salaries of Ministers who “are members of the Cabinet”;
  · recognised “Party”, “Opposition” and “the Leader of the Opposition”.
· However, the provisions of the Ministers of the Crown Act do not validate or legalise these conventions.
· What it does is to recognise them — that they exist.
· But once their existence is recognised by legislation, conventions do not really remain very different from laws.
· Jennings asserts that “the conventional system of the British Constitution is in fact much like the system of the common law.”

Topic 3.3 — Sanction Behind Conventions


3.3.1 The Central Question


· It is generally asked: Why are conventions so scrupulously observed in Britain?
· What is the sanction behind conventions, given that they are:
  · not enforced by courts,
  · not written down,
  · not enacted by Parliament?


3.3.2 Dicey's Explanation — Partial


· This has been partially explained by Dicey.
· His conclusion was that violation of conventions ultimately means breach of law.


Dicey's Example — Annual Session of Parliament


· Dicey takes the example of convening a session of Parliament every year.
· He argues:
  · If no session of Parliament is summoned annually, it is only a breach of convention, not a violation of law.
  · But if no session of Parliament is called annually, it is not possible to raise revenues and pass the Army and Air Force (Annual) Act.
  · In that case, it becomes illegal to maintain army and air force on money raised from unauthorised taxes.
  · Anyone doing so can be brought before a court for breach of law and punished accordingly.
· Therefore, it becomes essential rather imperative that Parliament should be summoned at least once a year.
· If it is not, it means indirect collision with the laws of the land.
· Similarly, the Ministry may come to grief if it does not resign after it has lost the confidence of the House of Commons.


3.3.3 Lowell's Criticism of Dicey


· But this does not cover the whole case.
· Lowell has correctly pointed out that:
  · Britain is not obliged for ever to hold annual sessions of Parliament.
  · Being a sovereign body, Parliament can pass a permanent Army and Air Force Act and grant the existing annual taxes for a number of years.
· Moreover, there are some conventions the violation of which does not necessarily lead to breach of law.


Examples Where Violation Does Not Lead to Breach of Law


· No breach of law would follow if:
  · the Speaker does not resign from the membership of his party after his election to that office;
  · the Government does not recognise His or Her Majesty's Opposition;
  · all the conventions relating to the conduct of business in the House of Commons are not observed;
  · the Prime Minister is taken from the House of Lords.


3.3.4 Precedents May Be Broken


· At the same time, precedents may be broken if the altered political conditions of the country demand that.
· Example — 1931 Labour Government:
  · The Labour Government violated the convention of ministerial collective responsibility when members of the Cabinet in 1931 "agreed to differ".
  · It was justified by Baldwin, who maintained that conventions were altered by circumstances.
· Example — Disraeli (1868):
  · Disraeli disregarded the well-established usage by resigning without meeting Parliament on defeat at the general election.
· Example — Baldwin (1929):
  · In 1929, Baldwin reverted to the old convention and considered it wholly constitutional for him to meet Parliament and receive its verdict.


3.3.5 Jennings' View — Conventions Exist for Good Reasons


· Jennings points out that conventions:
  · "do not exist for their own sake; they exist because there are good reasons for them."
· The good reason is that conventions are related to the idea of constitutional government and democracy with which almost all Britishers find themselves in agreement.


3.3.6 Neumann's Observation


· Neumann succinctly remarks:
  · "This remarkable island race simply prefers to retain proven procedures when there is no particularly strong reason to adopt innovations,
  · and has thereby produced a system of time-honoured customs and conventions
  · which are observed because they are based not only on precedent but also on reasons."
· Neumann further writes:
  · "But since there is no constitution in any formal sense, 'unconstitutional' means, in effect, only one thing — namely, that it is not proper.
  · This brings us to the core of the British system of government which is not a constitutional document, nor an elaborate system of checks and balances,
  · but rather the generally held and clearly understood belief that certain things simply are not done by gentlemen."


3.3.7 Jennings on Government as Co-operative Function


· "Government," according to Jennings, "is a co-operative function, and rules of law alone cannot provide for common action."
· It implies integration of the activities of many individuals.
· Each individual must follow certain rules if he is to play his part well.
· Rules are generally obeyed because of the habit to obey them, no matter whether they are laws or conventions.
· Conventions are, therefore:
  · rules of political behaviour,
  · first established to solve some specific problems,
  · subsequently followed as they seemed just and reasonable to follow.
· They established intelligent practices and continue their authority as such.


3.3.8 Dicey's View — Crown Converted into Privileges of the People


· Dicey's view is that the Crown shall be converted into the privileges of the people.
· "Our modern code of constitutional morality," he observed, "secures though in a roundabout way that is called abroad the sovereignty of the people."


3.3.9 Jennings on Political Difficulties


· It is in this context that Jennings wrote that:
  · "conventions are obeyed because of the political difficulties which follow if they are not."


3.3.10 Marshall and Moodie — Matter-of-Fact Explanation


· Marshall and Moodie give a more matter-of-fact explanation.
· They say:
  · "conventions describe the way in which certain legal powers must be exercised if the powers are to be tolerated by those affected."


3.3.11 Judicial Committee of the Privy Council — British Coal Corporation v. Rex (1935)


· This fact has been clearly expressed by the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in British Coal Corporation v. Rex (1935) when it interpreted the Statute of Westminster, 1931.
· The Committee declared:
  · "The Imperial Parliament could as a matter of abstract law, repeal or disregard Section 4 of the Statute. But that is theory and has no relation to realities."

Topic 3.4 — Kinds of Conventions


3.4.1 Three (or Four) Kinds of Conventions


· Conventions are essentially of three kinds:
  1. Those which ensure harmony between Parliament and the Executive in the light of Parliamentary Sovereignty (Cabinet Government).
  2. Those which relate to legislative procedure and the relations between the two Houses of Parliament.
  3. Those which aim at securing harmony between government and legislative action, on the one hand, and the verdict of the electorate, on the other (including the mandate convention).
· A fourth type (dealt with separately in the text) determines the relations between the Dominions and the United Kingdom.


---


3.4.1 — Conventions Ensuring Harmony Between Parliament and Executive (Cabinet Government)


Background — Glorious Revolution of 1688


· The Glorious Revolution of 1688 settled once for all that Parliament had supreme power and it could control every aspect of national life.
· The powers of the King were limited.
· Constitutional development led to the emergence of the Cabinet.
· Convention alone provides for the essential rules of the Cabinet Government.


Rules of the Cabinet Government (Conventions)


· Ministers of the King must be members of Parliament.
· They should belong to the majority party in the House of Commons.
· They should function under the party leader designated as the Prime Minister.
· The Cabinet is responsible to Parliament for its actions.
· It remains in office so long as it retains the confidence of the House of Commons.
· If the majority is reduced to minority and the Commons withdraw their support:
  · the Cabinet either resigns, or
  · appeals to the electorate for a mandate.
· The Ministry must resign if the verdict of the electorate is against it, allowing the Party in Opposition to form the government.
· If there are more opposing parties than one and the general election does not give a clear majority to one single party:
  · it may meet Parliament and allow a vote of the House of Commons to decide its fate (as the Conservative Ministry did in 1924);
  · but it cannot ask another dissolution, nor should the Crown concede it if asked.
· Conventions insist on the collective responsibility of Cabinet to Parliament for all its public acts.
· Its duty is to initiate legislation.
· The Ministry should combat domestic crisis with the authority at its disposal, but it must summon Parliament immediately to consult with it.
· The Ministry shall have full regard to the will of the Commons in the conduct of foreign affairs.
· It "shall not declare war, or neutrality or make peace, or enter into important treaties without securing as soon as possible endorsement by the Commons".
· The Commons should, so far as possible, be taken into counsel before the Crown is committed to any definite course of action.


---


3.4.2 — Conventions Relating to Legislative Procedure and Relations Between the Two Houses


Basic Conventions


· Parliament meets annually — rests on custom.
· Parliament consists of two Houses — rests on custom.


Financial Initiative and Subordination of the Lords


· The essential principle of the initiative of the House of Commons in matters of finance, under the authority of the Cabinet.
· The subordination of the Lords rested solely on convention until the Parliament Act of 1911.
· The Act of 1911, as amended in 1949, put definite limitations on the legislative powers of the House of Lords, which had hitherto been regulated by convention only.


Judicial Function of the House of Lords


· The principle that no peer other than a Law Lord sits when the House of Lords is acting as a Court of Appeal is also customary.


Parliamentary Procedure Conventions


· Every Bill must have three readings before finally voted upon — a matter of convention.
· A speech from the Government benches is to be followed by a speech from the Opposition — a convention.
· The whole idea of His or Her Majesty's Opposition is a product of convention.
· The Speaker of the House of Commons should become a no-party man and must resign from the membership of his party on his election as Speaker — a convention.
· It was another convention till very recently that the retiring Speaker must be returned unopposed and he should be elected Speaker as many times as he pleases.


---


3.4.3 — Conventions Linking Government/Legislature with the Electorate (Mandate Convention)


Purpose


· These conventions aim at securing harmony between government and legislative action, on the one hand, and the verdict of the electorate, on the other.


The Mandate Convention


· Government should not initiate legislation of a controversial nature unless they have a mandate from the electorate.
· This is called the "mandate convention".
· It is a vindication of the principle of popular sovereignty.
· It makes necessary that any item of policy involving radical changes must have been a part of the programme on which government fought the previous election.
· If it was not, the Opposition should show by its action or inaction that this is not a matter of keen controversy.


Example — 1945 Labour Government and House of Lords


· The Conservative majority in the House of Lords in the years immediately following Labour victory in 1945 approved bills embracing measures such as nationalisation on the ground that Labour had received a mandate from the electorate.
· Labour Party's manifesto (1945) read: "we give clear notice that we will not tolerate obstruction of the people's will by the House of Lords."
· Viscount Cranborne, leader of the Conservative Party in the House of Lords, said:
  · "Whatever our personal views, we should frankly recognise that these proposals were put before the country at the recent General Election and that the people of this country, with full knowledge of these proposals, returned the Labour Party to power.
  · The Government may therefore, I think, fairly claim that they have a mandate to introduce these proposals.
  · I think it would be constitutionally wrong, when the country has so recently expressed its views, for this House to oppose proposals which have been definitely put before the electorate."


Nature of the Mandate Convention


· The idea of electoral mandates is by no means new, although the concept was much more vague with the Liberals and the Conservatives.
· But Labour had always believed since 1918 that a party should go to the electorate with a set of concrete proposals which, if successful, it is thereby mandated to put into practice.


Other Conventions of This Type


· The mandate convention does not apply only to legislation, but also to foreign policy.
· Another example: when an appeal to the electors goes against the Ministry, they are bound to retire from office and have no right to dissolve Parliament a second time.
· Behind these conventions, according to Greaves, there is something of a political sanction.


---


3.4.4 — Conventions Governing Dominion/Commonwealth Relations


Statute of Westminster, 1931


· The Statute of Westminster, 1931, embodies in a legal form the conventions which at one time regulated inter-Imperial relations.
· This gave a constitutional sanction to the legislative independence of the Dominions.


Continuing Conventional Methods


· But the methods of inter-Commonwealth co-operation are still essentially conventional.


Examples


· In matters relating to the Dominions, the King acts on the advice of the Ministers of the Dominion concerned, and not on that of his Ministers constituting the Government in Britain.
· The British Parliament does not pass any law for a Dominion unless it has been expressly authorised by the Dominion concerned to do so.
· The rules for making treaties by any part of the Dominions are still found in the Reports of the Imperial Conference in 1923, 1926 and 1930.
· The position of the Dominion Governor-General was determined by agreements at the Conferences of 1926 and 1930.
· The co-operative link between the Commonwealth countries and their functioning as a single organism are matters of common understanding and mutual agreements.

Topic 3.5 — Why Conventions Are Observed in Britain


3.5.1 The Central Question


· It is generally asked: Why are conventions so scrupulously observed in Britain?
· Several explanations have been offered by different writers.


---


3.5.2 Dicey's Explanation — Violation Means Breach of Law (Partly)


· Dicey partially explained this: violation of conventions ultimately means breach of law.
· Example: If Parliament is not summoned annually → revenues cannot be raised → Army and Air Force (Annual) Act cannot be passed → maintaining army on unauthorised taxes becomes illegal → breach of law.
· Similarly, if the Ministry does not resign after losing confidence, it may come to grief.


Lowell's Criticism of Dicey


· Lowell pointed out that Dicey's explanation does not cover the whole case:
  · Britain is not obliged for ever to hold annual sessions of Parliament.
  · Parliament, being sovereign, can pass a permanent Army and Air Force Act and grant taxes for a number of years.
  · Some conventions can be violated without any breach of law:
    · Speaker not resigning from his party;
    · Government not recognising HM Opposition;
    · Conventions on conduct of business not observed;
    · Prime Minister taken from the House of Lords.


---


3.5.3 Jennings' Explanation — Political Difficulties and Good Reasons


· Jennings points out that conventions "do not exist for their own sake; they exist because there are good reasons for them."
· The good reason is that conventions are related to the idea of constitutional government and democracy with which almost all Britishers find themselves in agreement.
· "Conventions are obeyed because of the political difficulties which follow if they are not."
· "Government is a co-operative function, and rules of law alone cannot provide for common action."
· Rules are generally obeyed because of the habit to obey them, whether laws or conventions.
· Conventions are rules of political behaviour, first established to solve specific problems, subsequently followed as they seemed just and reasonable.


---


3.5.4 Neumann's Explanation — "Not Done by Gentlemen"


· Neumann remarks:
  · "This remarkable island race simply prefers to retain proven procedures when there is no particularly strong reason to adopt innovations,
  · and has thereby produced a system of time-honoured customs and conventions
  · which are observed because they are based not only on precedent but also on reasons."
· Neumann further writes:
  · "Since there is no constitution in any formal sense, 'unconstitutional' means, in effect, only one thing — namely, that it is not proper.
  · This brings us to the core of the British system of government which is not a constitutional document, nor an elaborate system of checks and balances,
  · but rather the generally held and clearly understood belief that certain things simply are not done by gentlemen."


---


3.5.5 Dicey's Moral View — Constitutional Morality and Sovereignty of the People


· Dicey's view: the Crown shall be converted into the privileges of the people.
· "Our modern code of constitutional morality secures though in a roundabout way that is called abroad the sovereignty of the people."
· Conventions thus have a moral/democratic foundation.


---


3.5.6 Marshall and Moodie — Legal Powers Must Be Tolerated


· Marshall and Moodie give a more matter-of-fact explanation:
  · "Conventions describe the way in which certain legal powers must be exercised if the powers are to be tolerated by those affected."
· That is, conventions ensure that legal powers are exercised in a manner acceptable to those affected; otherwise those powers would not be tolerated.


---


3.5.7 Judicial Committee of the Privy Council — British Coal Corporation v. Rex (1935)


· The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in British Coal Corporation v. Rex (1935) interpreted the Statute of Westminster, 1931.
· It declared:
  · "The Imperial Parliament could as a matter of abstract law, repeal or disregard Section 4 of the Statute. But that is theory and has no relation to realities."
· This shows:
  · Legal theory (Parliament's abstract legal power) is different from political reality (conventions and political practice).
  · Conventions operate in the realm of political reality, not abstract law.

Topic 3.6 — Role of Conventions: Flexibility, Democratisation, Smooth Working of Government


3.6.1 Conventions Democratise the Executive


· Conventions have democratised the Executive by:
  · making Parliament the centre of gravity;
  · enabling the democratic system to operate in a unitary government.
· Parliamentary practices emerging out of this process of democratisation enable the Government and the Opposition to:
  · sit together,
  · discuss, and
  · work together for the development of national welfare.


3.6.2 Conventions Revolutionised the Judiciary


· Conventions have also revolutionised the Judiciary by making the Law Lords constitute the highest Court of Civil Appeal in Britain.


3.6.3 Conventions and Commonwealth Relations


· Conventions have enabled inter-Commonwealth relations and the collaboration of the member nations to the common advantage.


3.6.4 Conventions Are Not Static Like Laws


· Conventions are not static like laws.
· They "provide the flesh which clothes the dry bones of law."
· Consequently, conventions have enabled a rigid legal framework of government to keep an organic pace with the changing political ideas and needs of the people.


3.6.5 New Needs Demand New Emphasis


· New needs demand a new emphasis and a new orientation even when the law remains fixed.
· Men have to work the old law in order to satisfy the new needs.
· Conventions are the motive power of the British Constitution.
· They lubricate the machinery of government and keep the machine going more smoothly.
· In their absence:
  · the structure of government is sure to collapse;
  · the nature of the British Constitution might well be very different from what it is now.
· The British system is the best example of democracy, especially of parliamentary democracy.


3.6.6 Conventions and the Fulness of Life's Meanings


· Herman Finer remarks:
  · "No written constitution, any more than the ordinary law, can express the fulness of life's meanings and demands, because the human imagination, even at its most talented, falls far short of reality."
· The real constitution is a living body of general prescriptions carried into effect by living persons.


3.6.7 Conventions Are Flexible and Growing


· Conventions are flexible and growing.
· They can be easily adjusted to future requirements without creating a political stir which an amendment of the constitution creates.
· They harmonise relations where a purely legal solution of practical problems is impossible.


3.6.8 Conventions and the Transition from Monarchy to Democracy


· "In converting a monarchical into a democratic constitution, and in passing from the seventeenth to the twentieth century, the British eschewed writing the new articles: they preferred to rely on the growth and inheritance of customs — that is, conventions."

Topic 3.7 — Limits of Dicey's Theory and Examples of Convention Violation/Change


3.7.1 Dicey's Theory — Recap


· Dicey's explanation: violation of conventions ultimately means breach of law.
· Example: No annual session of Parliament → no revenues → no Army and Air Force (Annual) Act → maintaining army on unauthorised taxes becomes illegal → breach of law.
· Dicey's conclusion: conventions are ultimately backed by legal sanctions.


3.7.2 Limits of Dicey's Theory — Lowell's Criticism


· Lowell correctly pointed out that Dicey's theory does not cover the whole case:
  · Britain is not obliged for ever to hold annual sessions of Parliament.
  · Parliament, being a sovereign body, can pass a permanent Army and Air Force Act and grant the existing annual taxes for a number of years.
  · In that case, not summoning Parliament annually would not lead to any breach of law.


3.7.3 Conventions Whose Violation Does Not Lead to Breach of Law


· There are some conventions the violation of which does not necessarily lead to breach of law:


Convention If Violated — No Breach of Law
Speaker resigns from party membership after election No breach of law if he does not resign
Government recognises HM Opposition No breach of law if not recognised
Conventions on conduct of business in the House of Commons No breach of law if not observed
Prime Minister taken from the House of Commons (by convention) No breach of law if taken from the House of Lords


· In all these cases, no legal consequence follows from violation.
· Yet these conventions are generally observed.


3.7.4 Precedents May Be Broken — Altered Political Conditions


· Precedents may be broken if the altered political conditions of the country demand that.
· Conventions are not rigid; they can be altered by circumstances.
· This shows the flexibility of conventions.


Example 1 — 1931 Labour Government: "Agreed to Differ"


· The Labour Government violated the convention of ministerial collective responsibility when members of the Cabinet in 1931 "agreed to differ".
· It was justified by Baldwin, who maintained that conventions were altered by circumstances.
· This shows that conventions can be modified or suspended in exceptional circumstances.


Example 2 — Disraeli (1868): Resigned Without Meeting Parliament


· Disraeli, in 1868, disregarded the well-established usage by:
  · resigning without meeting Parliament on defeat at the general election.
· This was a departure from the established convention.


Example 3 — Baldwin (1929): Reverted to the Old Convention


· In 1929, Baldwin reverted to the old convention and considered it wholly constitutional for him to:
  · meet Parliament and receive its verdict.
· This shows that conventions can be restored after being broken.


3.7.5 The Flexibility of Conventions


· One of the merits of conventions is the flexibility they impart in the governance of the country.
· Conventions can be adjusted to changing circumstances without the formal process of constitutional amendment.
· This flexibility is both a strength and a source of uncertainty (since conventions are not codified).


3.7.6 Jennings — Conventions Exist for Good Reasons


· Jennings points out that conventions:
  · "do not exist for their own sake; they exist because there are good reasons for them."
· The good reason is that conventions are related to the idea of constitutional government and democracy with which almost all Britishers find themselves in agreement.
· Conventions are not arbitrary; they serve a purpose.


3.7.7 Neumann — "Not Done by Gentlemen"


· Neumann succinctly remarks:
  · "This remarkable island race simply prefers to retain proven procedures when there is no particularly strong reason to adopt innovations,
  · and has thereby produced a system of time-honoured customs and conventions
  · which are observed because they are based not only on precedent but also on reasons."
· Neumann further writes:
  · "Since there is no constitution in any formal sense, 'unconstitutional' means, in effect, only one thing — namely, that it is not proper.
  · This brings us to the core of the British system of government which is not a constitutional document, nor an elaborate system of checks and balances,
  · but rather the generally held and clearly understood belief that certain things simply are not done by gentlemen."


3.7.8 Summary — Limits of Dicey's Theory


Limit Explanation
Parliament can pass permanent laws Not obliged to hold annual sessions; can grant taxes for years; no breach of law if not summoned
Some conventions violated without legal breach Speaker not resigning from party; not recognising Opposition; PM from House of Lords; conduct of business not observed
Precedents may be broken Altered political conditions may demand it (1931 Labour "agreed to differ"; Disraeli 1868; Baldwin 1929)
Conventions are flexible Can be altered by circumstances; not rigid like laws
Jennings Conventions exist for good reasons; not arbitrary
Neumann "Unconstitutional" = "not proper"; things simply not done by gentlemen


3.7.9 Dicey's Theory — Partial Truth


· Dicey's theory is partially true:
  · Some conventions are backed by ultimate legal sanctions (e.g., annual session).
  · Violation of some conventions does ultimately lead to breach of law.
· But Dicey's theory is not universally true:
  · Not all conventions have legal sanctions behind them.
  · Some conventions can be violated without any legal consequence.
  · Conventions can be altered by circumstances.
· Therefore, Dicey's explanation is incomplete but not entirely wrong.

Lowell's criticism Dicey incomplete; Parliament can pass permanent laws; no annual session needed
No legal breach examples Speaker not resigning from party; not recognising Opposition; PM from Lords; conduct of business not observed
Precedents broken Altered political conditions may demand it
1931 Labour "Agreed to differ"; justified by Baldwin; conventions altered by circumstances
Disraeli 1868 Resigned without meeting Parliament; disregarded usage
Baldwin 1929 Reverted to old convention; met Parliament
Flexibility Merit of conventions; adjusted without constitutional amendment
Jennings Conventions exist for good reasons; not arbitrary
Neumann "Unconstitutional" = "not proper"; things not done by gentlemen
Dicey's theory Partially true but incomplete
