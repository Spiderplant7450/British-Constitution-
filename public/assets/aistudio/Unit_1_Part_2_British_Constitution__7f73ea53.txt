Topic 2.1 — Charters, Petitions, Statutes, and Constitutional Landmarks


2.1.1 Introduction — Seven Main Categories of Sources


· The sources from which the British Constitution is drawn are many and diverse.
· They may be divided into seven main categories:
  1. Great Charters, Petitions, Statutes and other landmarks
  2. Ordinary statutes dealing with suffrage, elections, public officials, etc.
  3. Judicial decisions and precedents
  4. Common Law principles
  5. Usages and conventions
  6. Commentaries by eminent writers
  7. Royal Prerogative
· Topic 2.1 deals with the first category.


2.1.2 The Great Charters, Petitions, Statutes and Landmarks


· These include:
  · Magna Carta (1215)
  · Petition of Rights (1628)
  · Act of Settlement (1701), as modified by the Abdication Act (1936)
  · Act of Union with Scotland (1707)
  · Great Reform Act (1832)
  · Parliament Act of 1911, as amended in 1949
  · Government of Ireland Act (1920)
  · Public Order Act (1936)
  · Ministers of the Crown Act (1937)
  · Representation of the People Act (1949)
  · Life Peers Act (1958)
  · Peerage Act (1963)
  · Statute of Westminster (1931)
  · Indian Independence Act (1947)
  · etc.


2.1.3 Most Are Acts of Parliament


· Most of these are Acts passed by Parliament.
· But a document like Magna Carta is considered part of the Constitution because:
  · it makes a great landmark in national history.
· Various Acts of Parliament “may, without undue violence to the facts, be regarded as in direct line of descent from Magna Carta.”


2.1.4 Elder William P.’s Description


· Elder William P. called:
  · Magna Carta,
  · the Petition of Rights, and
  · the Bill of Rights
  · as the “Bible of the British Constitution.”


2.1.5 Significant Feature — Product of Political Stress and Crisis


· One thing very significant about these Charters and Statutes is that they were:
  · the product of political stress and crisis;
  · they contain the terms of settlement of that crisis.
· They are part of the Constitution because of what they deal with.
· It is the context of the constitutional struggle within which they originated that they bear the impress of constitutional law.


2.1.6 Examples of Landmark Documents and Their Significance


Document Year Significance
Magna Carta 1215 Great landmark in national history; basis of constitutional liberty
Petition of Rights 1628 Product of constitutional struggle; terms of settlement
Bill of Rights 1689 Embodied constitutional rules and principles; limitations on King’s powers

Act of Union with Scotland 1707 Constitutional landmark

Parliament Act 1911 (amended 1949) Limited legislative powers of House of Lords
Government of Ireland Act 1920 Constitutional landmark

Ministers of the Crown Act 1937 Recognised Prime Minister, Cabinet, Party, Opposition, Leader of Opposition
Representation of the People Act 1949 Suffrage and elections
Life Peers Act 1958 Composition of House of Lords
Peerage Act 1963 Peerage reform
Statute of Westminster 1931 Legislative independence of Dominions
Indian Independence Act 1947 Constitutional change in Empire


2.1.7 Key Points to Remember


· These documents are part of the Constitution because of their subject-matter (what they deal with) and their historical significance.
· They are not all of the same date; they are scattered over centuries.
· They were made as and when needed — often in response to political stress and crisis.
· They represent terms of settlement of constitutional crises.
· The context of constitutional struggle gives them their constitutional character.
· They are landmarks in the direct line of descent from Magna Carta.
· Together with the Petition of Rights and the Bill of Rights, Magna Carta has been called the “Bible of the British Constitution.”

Topic 2.2 — Ordinary Statutes Dealing with Suffrage, Elections, and Public Officials


2.2.1 Who Passes These Statutes


· These are a good number of Statutes which Parliament has passed from time to time.
· They deal with:
  · suffrage (the right to vote),
  · the methods of election, and
  · the powers and duties of public officials, etc.


2.2.2 How They Differ from Constitutional Landmarks (Group One)


· Unlike the constitutional landmarks (Magna Carta, Petition of Rights, Act of Settlement, etc.), these Statutes are not the outcome of a constitutional struggle.
· They were passed:
  · as and when the exigencies of time demanded them, and
  · under the ordinary process of things.


2.2.3 Example — Laws Extending the Right to Vote


· None of the laws extending the right to vote, passed between 1867 and 1948, aroused popular excitement as the Reform Act of 1832 did.
· The Reform Act of 1832 was a product of intense popular agitation; these later statutes were ordinary legislative measures.
· This illustrates that these Statutes were routine, not crisis-driven.


2.2.4 Importance for Political Democracy


· Nonetheless, all these Statutes are vitally important for the development of political democracy.
· They have:
  · widened the franchise,
  · regulated elections, and
  · defined the powers and duties of public officials.


2.2.5 Constitutional Sense of the Nation


· Any attempt to repeal these Statutes would now be regarded as against the “constitutional sense” of the nation.
· That is, the nation has come to accept them as fundamental to its constitutional order, even though they were passed as ordinary laws.


2.2.6 System Would Become Unworkable Without Them


· In fact, the system of government obtainable in Britain would become unworkable if ever an attempt is made to repeal any one of such Statutes.
· This is despite the fact that Parliament is a sovereign body.
· Parliament has “the right to make or unmake any law whatever.”
· So, legally Parliament could repeal them, but politically and constitutionally it would be against the “constitutional sense” of the nation, and the system would collapse.


2.2.7 The Paradox — Legal Sovereignty vs. Political Reality


· Legal position: Parliament is sovereign; it can make or unmake any law.
· Political reality: These ordinary statutes are so fundamental that repeal would be constitutionally unthinkable and would make government unworkable.
· This shows the difference between legal theory and political practice in the British Constitution.


2.2.8 Summary Table

Topic 2.3 — Judicial Decisions and Precedents as a Source of the Constitution


2.3.1 The Third Source of Constitutional Rules


· The third source of constitutional rules is to be found in the decisions of judges on cases heard by them in the law courts.
· When judges decide cases, they interpret, define and develop the provisions of the great Charters and Statutes.
· While doing so, their judgments create precedents which succeeding judges respect.


2.3.2 Judicial Precedents as an Important Element


· Since many of these judgments related directly to constitutional matters, the legal principles and judicial precedents of these judgments are an important element in the British Constitution.
· They resemble and correspond to the decisions of the Supreme Court of the United States which have helped to clarify and expand the provisions of the American Constitution.


2.3.3 Key Case Examples


· Sheriff of Middlesex case (1840):
  · Established the principle that Parliament has the right to punish its own members for a breach of privilege;
  · no other legal authority being necessary.
· Bradlaugh v. Gossett (1884):
  · Established the supremacy of Parliament over the courts in all matters concerning the internal affairs of Parliament.


2.3.4 How Judicial Decisions Work as a Source


· Judges interpret statutes and charters.
· Their interpretations become precedents.
· Succeeding judges respect these precedents.
· Over time, a body of legal principles grows up around constitutional matters.
· This body of principles is an essential part of the British Constitution, even though it is not codified.


2.3.5 Comparison with the US Supreme Court


· The role of British judges in constitutional matters resembles the role of the US Supreme Court.
· The US Supreme Court’s decisions have clarified and expanded the American Constitution.
· Similarly, British judicial decisions have clarified and expanded the British Constitution.
· This shows that judicial decisions are a universal source of constitutional growth, not unique to Britain.

Aspect Explanation

Constitutional role Legal principles and precedents on constitutional matters form an important element of the Constitution

Topic 2.4 — Common Law Principles as a Source of the Constitution


2.4.1 The Fourth Source of Constitutional Rules


· The fourth source of constitutional rules is the principles of the Common Law and several matters of major constitutional importance covered by them.


2.4.2 What the Common Law Provides


· It is from the Common Law that:
  · the King derives his prerogative;
  · Parliament derives its supremacy.
· The civil liberties of the people, which in America are embodied in the Bill of Rights, are ensured in Britain by the rules of the Common Law.
· These include:
  · freedom of speech,
  · freedom of press,
  · freedom of assembly,
  · the sanctity of a citizen's home, and
  · the right of jury trial.
· These are Common Law rights which today have their effective meaning in the long line of decisions judges have made.


2.4.3 Relationship with Parliamentary Law


· The laws of Parliament may redefine or modify the manner of exercising these rights.
· But such laws are in their turn subject to judicial interpretation made in the light of the many precedents of the past.


2.4.4 Nature of Common Law — Based on Usage


· The principles of the Common Law are not established by any law passed by Parliament or ordained by the King.
· They grew up entirely on the basis of usage.


2.4.5 Blackstone's Definition


· According to Blackstone, Common Law consists of customs:
  · **"not set down in any written statute or ordinance,
  · but depending on immemorial usages for their support."**


2.4.6 How Common Law Grew


· The judges recognised "the customs of the realm",
· applied them in individual cases, and
· set precedents for decisions in later cases.
· As these decisions were "broadened down from precedent to precedent", there grew up:
  · a body of principles of general application,
  · which stand as a bulwark of British freedom and
  · an essential part of the British Constitution.


2.4.7 Common Law Is Continually Developing


· The Common Law, like statutory law, is:
  · "continually in the process of development by judicial decisions."

Topic 2.5 — Usages and Conventions as a Source of the Constitution


2.5.1 The Fifth Source of Constitutional Rules


· Another source of constitutional rules is to be found in usages or conventions.
· The conventions of the constitution, as they are called, are:
  · the centre and soul of the constitutional law in Britain.


2.5.2 The Fundamental Convention


· The fundamental convention, from which practically all others flow, is the convention of the Cabinet Government.
· This convention is the foundation of the entire system of responsible parliamentary government.


2.5.3 Nature of Conventions


· Although the validity of the conventions of the constitution cannot be the subject of proceedings in courts of law,
· yet they cover some of the most important parts of the British political system and are observed with due respect.
· Conventions are, as Herman Finer says:
  · "rules of political behaviour not established in statutes, judicial decisions or Parliamentary customs but created outside these, supplementing them, in order to achieve objects they have not yet embodied."


2.5.4 Objects of Conventions (Finer's Formulation)


· These objects, in the British Constitution, can be summed up thus:
  · to make the executive and the legislature responsible to the will of the people.
· To add concreteness, we could use:
  · Crown, Government, or Cabinet in place of Executive;
  · Parliament, meaning the House of Commons (especially) and the House of Lords, in place of Legislature.


2.5.5 Conventions Distinguished from Laws


· A distinction is very often made between laws of the Constitution and conventions of the Constitution.
· But conventions are not really very different from laws and it is frequently difficult to place a set of rules in one class or the other.
· Jennings has rightly said that the Conventions, like most fundamental rules of any Constitution, rest essentially upon general acquiescence.
· "A written constitution is not law because somebody has made it, but it has been accepted."
· Conventions are based on usage and acquiescence and their binding force, like laws, is derived from the willingness of the people to be so bound.
· If obedience to law is deemed a fundamental duty, obedience to conventions is among the political obligations, because they help the wheels of political machine going in accordance with the will of the people.
· Both law and conventions are inevitably similar as they serve the common purpose of regulating the structure and functions of government aiming at the good of the people and are the result of common consent.


2.5.6 Technical Differences Between Laws and Conventions


· Technically, the difference between laws and conventions spreads to three aspects:


Aspect Law Convention
Origin Emanates from a legally constituted body; carries greater sanctity Extra-legal; grows out of practice; existence determined by usage
Formulation Usually expressed in precise terms; has the dignity of extracting unquestioning obedience Never formulated; grows out of practice; modified by practice; difficult to say whether a practice has become a convention
Enforcement Enforced by courts; judges consider whether Acts are legally valid Not enforced by courts; judges cannot force obedience; no legal sanction


· But even from the technical point of view, no definite boundary line can be drawn between legislation and conventions.
· If a given provision is part of the British Constitution, it is either law or convention and the fundamental conventions have well-nigh been recognised by many Acts of Parliament.


2.5.7 Recognition of Conventions by Legislation


· Preamble to the British North America Act, 1867 (now Canada Act):
  · "the Provinces of... have expressed their desire to be federally united into one Dominion under the Crown of the United Kingdom... with a constitution similar in principle to that of the United Kingdom."
· Article 2 of the Agreement for a Treaty between Great Britain and Ireland in 1921:
  · the law, practice, and constitutional usage governing the relationship of the Crown or of its representatives, or of the Imperial Parliament to the Dominion of Canada, were made applicable to the Irish Free State.
· Section 4 of the Status of the Union Act, 1934 (Union Parliament):
  · specific reference to the constitutional conventions regulating the use by the Governor-General of his legal power of summoning and dissolving Parliament, and of appointing ministers.
· Preamble to the Statute of Westminster:
  · some conventions regulating relationship between the Dominions and the United Kingdom inserted;
  · particularly those relating to alterations in the law touching the Succession to the Throne, or the Royal Style and Titles;
  · and the legislative authority of the British Parliament.
· The importance of the first of these conventions was demonstrated in the abdication of Edward VIII.
· The change in the Royal Style and Titles after the Indian Independence Act, 1947 was brought about by the full assent of the Dominion Parliament.


2.5.8 Cabinet and Prime Minister — Recognised by Legislation


· The Cabinet system of Government presupposes the pre-eminence and leadership of one single person — the Prime Minister.
· Abolish the institution of Prime Minister or diminish any part of his powers, the entire political structure would be destroyed.
· Yet neither the institution of Cabinet nor the office of the Prime Minister were known to law before 1937.
· The Ministers of the Crown Act, 1937:
  · provided for the payment of a salary of £10,000 a year "to the person who is the Prime Minister and the First Lord of the Treasury";
  · provided for salaries of Ministers who "are members of the Cabinet";
  · recognised "Party", "Opposition" and "the Leader of the Opposition".
· However, the provisions of the Ministers of the Crown Act do not validate or legalise these conventions.
· What it does is to recognise them — that they exist.
· But once their existence is recognised by legislation, conventions do not really remain very different from laws.
· Jennings asserts that "the conventional system of the British Constitution is in fact much like the system of the common law."


2.5.9 Summary Table of Technical Differences


Feature Law Convention
Source Legally constituted body Practice/usage
Formulation Precise terms Never formulated
Enforcement Courts Not courts; political sanction
Sanctity Greater Less (but observed with respect)
Binding force Willingness of people Willingness of people (general acquiescence)
Purpose Regulate structure and functions of government for public good Same
Recognition Enacted by Parliament Recognised by many Acts of Parliament (e.g., Ministers of the Crown Act 1937)

Topic 2.6 — Commentaries by Eminent Writers as a Source of the Constitution


2.6.1 The Sixth Source of Constitutional Rules


· Next, but less reliable than the earlier sources, are the commentaries by eminent writers.
· Their works have come to be regarded as authoritative expressions on the British Constitutional Law.


2.6.2 What Commentators Have Done


· These commentators have:
  · systematised the diverse conventional rules;
  · established a definite relation of one to another;
  · linked them into some degree of unity by reference to central principles.
· In certain cases, such writers have provided compendiums and detailed accounts of the operation of particular categories of rules.
· Their works have acquired the status of constitutional documents.


2.6.3 Erskine May’s Treatise


· Probably the most authoritative of such works is Erskine May’s Treatise on the Law, Privileges, Proceedings and Usages of Parliament.
· It is the classic guide to the procedure and privileges of Parliament.
· It is constantly referred to by the Speakers of the House of Commons on the formulation of their rulings on questions of privilege and procedure.


2.6.4 A.V. Dicey’s Law of the Constitution


· A.V. Dicey’s Law of the Constitution has acquired over the years an authority that makes it more than merely a commentary on constitutional practice.
· (Although to a much lesser extent than Erskine May, according to the text.)


2.6.5 Nature of This Source


· Commentaries are less reliable than statutes, judicial decisions, Common Law, and conventions.
· But they are still an important source because:
  · they systematise rules;
  · they are treated as authoritative;
  · they are relied upon by practitioners (e.g., Speakers of the House of Commons).
· They have acquired the status of constitutional documents, even though they are not enacted by Parliament or decided by courts.

Topic 2.7 — Royal Prerogative as a Source of the Constitution


2.7.1 The Seventh Source


· Finally, the exercise of the Royal Prerogative forms another aspect of constitutional practice.
· It is the seventh source of the British Constitution.


2.7.2 Important Functions Performed by Royal Prerogative


· The power to:
  · declare war,
  · make treaties,
  · pardon criminals, and
  · dissolve Parliament
  · are important functions performed by Royal Prerogative.


2.7.3 How These Functions Are Executed


· They are executed through:
  · Orders in Council, or
  · through proclamations and writs under the great seal.


2.7.4 Modern Practice — Ministers Act on Behalf of the Monarch


· Today, these functions are performed by Ministers on behalf of the Monarch.
· As such, the authority for the decision comes from the Crown rather than Parliament.
· This means that even though the Monarch personally does not exercise these powers, the legal authority still flows from the Crown (the Royal Prerogative), not from Parliament.


2.7.5 Nature of the Royal Prerogative


· The Royal Prerogative is a source of constitutional rules because:
  · it confers legal powers on the Crown (exercised by Ministers);
  · it covers major constitutional matters (war, treaties, pardons, dissolution);
  · it operates through established legal forms (Orders in Council, proclamations, writs under the great seal).
· It is not codified in a single statute; it is part of the unwritten constitution.

Topic 2.8 — Summary of the Nature of the British Constitution (Anson and Bagehot)


2.8.1 Anson’s Summary


· Anson summed up the nature of the British Constitution in these words:
  · It is “a somewhat rambling structure”.
  · Like a house which many successive owners have altered just so far as suited their immediate wants or fashion of the time.
  · It bears the marks of many hands.
  · It is convenient rather than symmetrical.
  · Forms and phrases survive which have long since lost their meaning.
  · The adaptation of practice to convenience by a process of unconscious change has brought about in many cases a divergence of law and custom, of theory and practice.


2.8.2 Key Elements of Anson’s Description


Element Meaning
Rambling structure Not neatly planned; grown piecemeal
Many successive owners Many generations have altered it
Immediate wants / fashion Changes made for practical needs, not abstract theory
Marks of many hands Many authors and influences
Convenient rather than symmetrical Practical utility over logical symmetry
Forms/phrases survive without meaning Anomalies and survivals remain
Divergence of law and custom Legal rules and actual practice differ
Divergence of theory and practice What is written/legal differs from what actually happens


2.8.3 Bagehot’s Prerequisites


· Walter Bagehot in his classic work The English Constitution asserted that such a system of government as obtainable in Britain was possible because there existed certain prerequisites:
  1. Mutual confidence among electors
  2. A calm national mind
  3. The gift of rationality
· All these qualities add up to an adult and practical nation.


2.8.4 Bagehot’s Conclusion


· The obvious result is that:
  · “the British for the most part think that the nature of their Constitution is most sensible”
  · and that “a codified constitution like the American is more trouble than it is worth.”


2.8.5 Significance of These Summaries


· Anson highlights:
  · the historical, piecemeal, practical character of the Constitution;
  · its lack of symmetry;
  · the divergence between law and custom, theory and practice.
· Bagehot highlights:
  · the social and psychological preconditions of the British system;
  · the practical wisdom of the British people;
  · their preference for an uncodified constitution.


2.8.6 Combined Picture


· Together, Anson and Bagehot present the British Constitution as:
  · historically grown;
  · practical and convenient;
  · not logically symmetrical;
  · dependent on certain national qualities (confidence, calmness, rationality);
  · deliberately uncodified because the British prefer it that way.
